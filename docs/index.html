Agent RL  ·  Preliminary results

Collective Intelligence: Preliminary Results

Before training a team of agents, I ran the groundwork: harness baselines on a 27B model, an RL run on a 9B model, an agentic RL arm, and the infrastructure to make all of it affordable. These are the results, and what each one taught me.

Models · Qwen3.8-27B (baselines) · Qwen3.5-9B (RL) Tasks · FrontierSmith → FrontierCS · NL2Repo · KernelBench · Toolathlon · EnterpriseOps-Gym Stack · vLLM · verl / FSDP2 · Qwen Code · GRPO
Findings so far
  1. Untrained, the harness mode doesn't matter. Solo, built-in subagents, team mode and three versions of my collective harness are all within noise on 96 tasks (one attempt each). Collaboration appears only where the work splits by module, and the best "collective" number came from a single agent using a visible clock.
  2. Single-call GRPO nearly doubled FrontierCS. Qwen3.5-9B went from 6.22 → 12.22 on 172 problems by step 70, paired +6.00 [+4.00, +8.33]. Most of the gain came by step 40, and it flattened as the policy fit a 200-problem training pool.
  3. The same recipe inside a general coding harness learned nothing. At step 10 the change was −0.02 [−1.01, +0.90]. The harness cut the model's thinking to 27 tokens per call.
  4. Agentic RL costs about 8× more per step, and most of that gap is fixable infrastructure. The big levers were admission control (about 10×), feeding the model its own token ids (2.7× faster updates), multi-token-prediction speculative decoding (1.9×), and a judge farm sized so scores don't silently degrade.
  5. The dangerous failures are silent. These include judges that return wrong scores without erroring, advantage scaling that amplifies noise, gradients that diverge under parallelism, and graders a policy could game.
Epistemic status

No team has been trained yet. The 27B baselines are single attempts per task, which vary by up to 0.25, so they describe behaviour rather than establish effects. The RL results are paired over the same problems with bootstrap intervals. The collective-intelligence design is summarised in §7, clearly marked as a plan.

Snapshot of work through October 10, 2026.

01 · Result 1 · Untrained 27B

Solo, subagents, a team, or a collective: on single attempts, nothing wins

Before training anything, I wanted to know what an off-the-shelf model does with teammates. I ran Qwen3.8-27B inside the Qwen Code agent harness on a 96-task eval set in six arms. Three are the harness's own modes: solo, built-in subagents (a lead delegates pieces to helpers with fresh contexts), and agent-team mode (a leader plus up to three teammates and a shared task list). The other three are versions of a collective harness I built, in which any agent can fork a copy of itself and agents talk peer to peer (§7 describes it briefly). Every arm used the same model, sampling, 262K context window, compaction rule and native time or step caps, with one attempt per task.

The eval set was chosen to be hard for a single agent. I picked tasks only from published per-task difficulty, never from my own runs: low but nonzero scores for comparable models, and often failed by frontier agents. I dropped anything that overlaps the training pools, for example ten FrontierCS problems that are near-copies of training problems.

So the honest headline is a null: on these tasks, an untrained 27B model doesn't get better by being given teammates, in either the harness's built-in team modes or mine. Three behaviours behind the numbers are more informative than the means.

Collaboration only appears where the work splits

In the collective harness the model never forked on tool workflows, enterprise tasks or GPU kernels. It forked about once in ten trials on FrontierCS, where the deliverable is a single optimised program. On NL2Repo, which means building a multi-module package from a spec, it forked in 15 of 22 trials, made 44 forks, and ran up to six agents at once. The work divides by module, so the model divides it.

The structure it built was always the same, and nobody told it to: all 47 forks came from the first agent, one level deep. That agent made itself the lead, wrote a brief for each worker into the workspace, forked workers in waves, ran the full test suite and sent failures back to whoever owned the module. Workers did talk to each other: they warned about broken imports, and once a worker noticed it had the same assignment as another, picked up an unowned test harness instead, and told both. In that trial the team scored 0.486 against 0.176 solo. But the lead also spent a lot of time idle, waiting on messages, and across NL2Repo the forking trials beat solo by only +0.03.

The best collective result came from one agent and a clock

The second version of the harness gave every agent its own git worktree and showed time remaining in every tool result. Its best number, 0.309 vs 0.251 solo on 3-hour FrontierCS, came from trials where the first agent worked alone and simply used the time: its median submission moved from about 43 to about 75 minutes. At 1 hour, the visible clock flipped behaviour the other way. Submissions rose from 17 to 37 of 40 trials, and runs that hit the time cap fell from 16 to 2. A visible timer changes more than any collaboration primitive did.

The submit action was adopted immediately, as a "done" button. That cost points: two FrontierCS submissions at minutes 4 and 27 scored 0, and four trials submitted while workers were still running.

Long tasks are compaction tasks

Between 77% and 90% of 1-hour trials compacted their context at least once, in every arm, some up to five times, and the median peak prompt was about 225K tokens. At this length "solo vs team" is partly "one lossy memory vs several". That's the regime where a team should help, and one more reason a single-attempt null is a starting point, not a verdict.

Other things this baseline caught
  • Lighter load, not delegation, explained a gain. The subagents arm on FrontierCS decoded at 167 tokens/s against solo's 148, because it ran with less load. Trials that never called a subagent gained as much as trials that did.
  • Forking slows decoding. NL2Repo collective trials ran at 114 tokens/s against 149 solo, which costs real work under a time cap.
  • A 32K per-call output cap cut solo FrontierCS in half. 51% of output tokens were in truncated calls; with the cap fixed, the score went from 0.108 to 0.215.
  • Team mode stranded work. In two NL2Repo tasks, teammates' packages were left in worktrees the grader never saw, and the tasks scored 0.
  • One collective trial scored 0.551 where every other arm scored 0. On a 3-hour FrontierCS problem, the lead built test tools, forked an "integrator" and an "optimiser", and checked every merge with sanitizers and brute force. It didn't recur in the next version.

The untrained model has the mechanics of teamwork (forking, briefs, peer fixes, integration testing) but not the judgment of when it pays. That judgment is what RL is supposed to teach.

02 · Result 2 · 9B post-training

GRPO on FrontierSmith nearly doubled FrontierCS, then flattened

A team-RL result would mean nothing if the RL stack couldn't improve a single model, so the first training run was solo and as simple as possible. I trained Qwen3.5-9B with single-turn GRPO on the 200 FrontierSmith training problems and evaluated it on all 172 algorithmic FrontierCS problems with the benchmark's own partial-credit judge (0–100), 5 samples per problem.

For reference, the published FrontierSmith recipe reports 1.80 → 10.62 for this model after 100 steps at a 16K-token budget. In that setting my base model scores 1.19; at my 64K budget it starts at 6.22. So the comparable number is the gain, not the endpoint.

A 16K budget starves GRPO

At the published 16K-token cap, 98% of my responses were truncated and every reward was 0. When every sample in a group scores the same, the group-relative advantage is zero and there is nothing to learn. At 64K, step-1 reward was about 0.15, with signal in 6 of 8 groups.

Teaching it to stop thinking

Even at 64K, more than half of the responses ran out of budget mid-thought. Forcing a </think> at a budget didn't work: the model kept reasoning in the answer channel (median about 22K more tokens) and often wrote a second </think>. What worked was closing the way the model itself does. At a 56K thinking budget, the loop injects the model's own early-exit sentence plus an opening code fence, masks both from the loss, and caps the answer at 8K tokens. A second </think> dropped from 40 to 9 of 64 samples at step 1. The new cap did not make the base model better (−0.50 [−1.54, +0.51]), so the later gain is training, and much of it is the model learning to finish. In the base model, samples whose thinking closed naturally scored 11.1 on average against 1.75 for force-closed ones.

Keep loops and failures in the batch

About 14% of base-model responses loop, typically detectable only around 50K tokens in. Dropping them would hide looping from the gradient and bias the group baseline upward, so loops are stopped early and kept as failures. Retries are only for infrastructure failures, and those are masked, never scored as 0. I also dropped an overlong-response penalty that would have dominated the signal: mean step-1 reward was −0.62 with the penalty, while task scores were around 0.1.

Why it flattened

With 200 training problems and 8 problems per step, each problem had been drawn about 5.5 times by step 70. On repeat draws, training reward roughly doubled (0.136 → 0.303), and groups where all eight samples scored the same rose from 1% to 10%. Those groups carry no gradient. Held-out gains stopped while training reward kept rising: the policy was fitting a small pool. I stopped the run at step 77 and kept the step-40 and step-70 checkpoints.

One detail matters for anyone reaching for an entropy-preserving fix. With one optimizer update per step, the importance ratio is exactly 1. The PPO clip never fires (clip fraction 0.0 at every step), so clip-higher and KL-Cov are no-ops: KL-Cov produced bit-identical updates. Fixes that act on the gradient directly, such as Clip-Cov's token masking, or more problems, are the levers that are left.

The plateau that wasn't, earlier

For quick checks I also kept a 40-problem subset. On it, step 20 looked no better than step 10 (−0.16 [−1.35, +1.06]), while on all 172 problems it was clearly better (+1.40 [+0.05, +2.87]). The subset has only 11–16 problems the model can partly solve, too few to resolve a trend. It's useful as a smoke test, not for deciding whether training is working.

The single-call recipe
  • GRPO with std-normalised group advantages, group size 8, 8 groups per step. Dynamic sampling keeps only groups with a reward spread (12 prompts at a time, up to 3 rounds).
  • Reward is the task score with no length term; one optimizer update per step.
  • PPO clip 0.2 with dual clip 3, sequence-mean-token-mean, no entropy bonus, KL loss 0.001 (low-variance KL) to the frozen base.
  • AdamW, lr 1e-6 constant, weight decay 0.01, grad clip 1.0.
  • Sampling at T 1.0, top-p 1.0. Evaluation uses the same thinking cap: 5 samples per problem, Avg@5 and Best@5.

03 · Result 3 · The agentic arm

Inside a general-purpose coding harness, the same recipe learned nothing

A team has to run inside an agent harness, so the next step was the same model, problems and judge, trained as a solo agent in a coding harness (Qwen Code). That meant a 262K context window, many short model calls per trial, and deadlines of 15–30 minutes. The recipe was token-level GRPO with a standard-deviation floor on the advantage, KL 0.01 to the base model, one update per batch, and parallel dynamic sampling (launch 12 groups × 8 trials, keep the first 8 groups with a reward spread). Only the model's own sampled tokens were trained.

Training looked healthy: rewards 0.18–0.33, gradient norm about 0.15, no dropped trajectories. The eval disagreed.

KL to the base model stayed flat at about 0.0003, while the single-call arm's kept rising (0.0015 at step 20, 0.0026 at step 30). Changing the KL coefficient would have been the reflexive fix, but at that drift the KL term's pull is about a thousand times weaker than the reward's, so it was the wrong lever. Reading trajectories found the problem. On the same packing puzzle:

  • The single-call model at step 40 thought for about 30K tokens, worked out that the problem is NP-hard, designed a best-fit greedy over all eight rotations and reflections, wrote one program, and scored 16.9.
  • The agentic model at step 10 made 178 calls and scored 0. Each call carried one sentence of narration as its "thinking". It rewrote its solution from scratch about ten times, once writing JavaScript into a .cpp file, called tools that don't exist, and "verified" an output that placed nothing.

Across agentic evals, thinking was a median of 27 tokens per call, and in 3.5% of trials the agent deleted its own solution during cleanup. The likely causes are all harness design:

  • The problem arrived as a file to read rather than as the question, so the model treated it as a quick tool result.
  • The roughly 13K-token system prompt is written for software engineering ("briefly state what you're about to do").
  • The agent had no local scorer, although the benchmark's own agent mode provides one.

I stopped the run and replaced the general-purpose harness with a minimal loop of my own:

  • the statement goes in the first message;
  • a handful of tools, plus a submit that runs the benchmark's feedback script;
  • exact token ids captured on every call;
  • the deliverable is protected from deletion.

In a first smoke test, the base model in that loop still thinks briefly per call (median 41 tokens), but its planning call now runs to a few thousand tokens, and it submits 16–39 times per trial against real feedback. Whether that trains is the next result.

A healthy training dashboard can sit on top of a policy that isn't learning anything useful. Read what the model actually does, and treat the harness as part of the model.

04 · AI infrastructure

Making agent RL affordable: what actually moved throughput

Agentic RL is expensive in a way single-call RL isn't. Per training step, the agentic arm used about 8× the GPU-hours of the single-call arm while generating about the same number of tokens. Most of this project's engineering went into closing that gap without touching what the model learns. Two rules made that safe:

  • Push speed until the next lever is worth less than 5%, then launch. Don't optimise during a long run.
  • An infrastructure-only change must leave the gradient unchanged, and that has to be shown by replay: the same kept groups, gradient cosine at the noise floor, byte-identical batches. Speed changes are also measured off the live run, because faster serving changes how many turns fit before a deadline.

Serving many long-context agents

Admission control was the single biggest lever: about 10×. On the 27B at 262K context, 22 concurrent agents at about 192K tokens each need roughly 4.2M tokens of KV cache, more than the 3.5M available. Agents take turns cyclically, and LRU eviction meets that pattern in the worst way: the prefix needed next is always the one just evicted. The prefix-cache hit rate fell from 97% to 1–4%, every turn re-prefilled ~190K tokens, and in a two-hour pilot 70 of 73 trials hit the time cap after about 130 tool calls. Healthy runs make 367–893 calls in four hours. Capping concurrent trials at the number of slots the cache can hold (12–14 per replica) restored throughput about tenfold.

Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, so adding KV capacity doesn't add throughput. Offloading KV to CPU memory stopped the thrashing: evicted prefixes reload at about 118K tokens/s. But unit throughput stayed flat (38–44 turns/min) while per-agent speed fell from 4.4 to 2.2 turns/min as agents were added. Each decode step reads about 12 GB of KV per 190K-token agent, and that bandwidth is the ceiling. I kept offload as a safety margin, not a speedup.

FP8 KV cache: twice the capacity, fine quality, still slower. Capacity went from 3.1M to 6.0M tokens. Quality held: top-1 agreement 99.1% with BF16 and NLL +0.2% at 81K, though +1.2% at 164K, so the error grows with context. Yet at equal load it was 13% slower, and doubling the agents to fill the extra capacity halved each agent's speed. A bandwidth model predicted 1.4–1.6× faster. The likely culprits are FP8 prefill at this head size and a doubled cache-block alignment. I kept BF16. Capacity only pays when preemption is the bottleneck, and here it wasn't.

Speculative decoding with the model's own multi-token-prediction head gave 1.9× agent turns per minute on the 27B (2 draft tokens), and on the 9B a call without it is about 1.9× slower. It's lossless for RL: the trainer-vs-rollout log-prob gap didn't change.

Agents per GPU has a knee. On the 9B, going from 12 to 18 agents per GPU raised GPU output only 5% (850 → 896 tok/s) while every trial got 32–44% slower, so about 12 is the knee. Prefix-cache hits stay around 97% up to 16 agents per replica and fall to 63–67% at 32.

The best tensor-parallel layout flips between workloads. For 48 concurrent 9B agents on 4 GPUs, four 1-GPU replicas beat one 4-GPU replica by 29% (976 vs 754 tok/s per GPU). For single-call RL rollout, a batch of long generations on one engine, a single 4-GPU engine was about 20% faster than four 1-GPU engines. For the 27B at 262K context the 4-GPU layout wins again, because each 1-GPU replica holds too little KV per agent.

Hybrid models need their prefix cache configured. On a model with linear-attention layers, the inference engine by default kept the recurrent state only at prompt boundaries. A resubmitted prompt-plus-output reused 10.6% of its tokens; keeping state at every cache block raised that to 90.3%, with the same decode speed and identical greedy output. I also compared two inference engines on the same GPU: one decoded 8–22% faster and held 2.7× more KV, while the other ignored the thinking budget when speculative decoding was on. That settled it.

Between rollout and trainer

Feed the model its own tokens back. An agent harness talks to the server in text, and the server re-tokenises it. At temperature 1 the model sometimes samples a non-canonical token split, and when that text comes back in the next prompt the ids no longer match what was generated. The training sequence has to break there and re-process the prefix. About 3.3% of calls had such a split, and that alone made the trainer process 2.71× the unique tokens. Most of the waste came from the tokenizer round trip; a smaller share came from the chat template and tool-call parser re-rendering things (false came back as False). Passing token ids in and out instead of text brought overhead to 1.05×. The trainer update went from 3,184 s to 1,171 s, the step from about 89 to about 47 minutes, at 73 ms extra per call. Training is now exactly on-policy for context. Because this changes what the model sees, the base model had to be re-evaluated under the new path before comparing checkpoints.

Stop surplus rollouts early. Dynamic sampling launches more groups than it keeps. Cancelling the surplus as soon as enough groups with a reward spread have finished cut step time 20% (2,598 → 2,085 s), and the kept set was identical on 14 replayed steps. Rewards computed under rollout load only decide when to stop; the kept groups are graded again once load settles, because a judge under heavy CPU load lowered 3 of 96 scores.

Overlap training with rollout without going off-policy. A one-step-stale asynchronous design would have nearly halved step time, but it changes the algorithm. Instead the trainer accumulates gradients per group as each group finishes. That is mathematically the same update, matching the batch path to a relative L2 of 2.5e-8 once losses are pre-scaled by a power of two. The time the trainer still waits after the last group fell from 871 s to 460 s.

Small things on the critical path add up. Background, per-rank checkpoint writes took the save on the critical path from 43.5 s to 8–11 s, with bit-identical weights. Parallel per-shard weight sync to rollout servers took 31–37 s down to 10–13 s. Grading inside the generation loop hid judge time almost completely. A chunked loop-detector cost 20–35% of generation for a 0.4% hit rate, so it was turned off.

More GPUs isn't always cheaper. The trainer scaled 1.88–1.90× from 4 to 8 GPUs, about 95% efficiency. But on a small single-call job, 4 GPUs used 31% fewer GPU-hours per step than 8, because generation, not the update, dominated. Memory had its own trap: Adam's state only exists after the first optimizer step, so a run that fits at step 1 can run out of memory at step 2 (§5).

Evaluation and grading

Size the judge farm by CPU, and know where scores start to degrade. A programmatic judge for competitive programming costs about 20 s of CPU per submission (median 6 s, p90 32 s), and many problems have time limits. Oversubscribing cores doesn't fail loudly. It lowers scores: 16 parallel test cases on 16 cores lowered 7 of 144 scores, while 12 lowered none. The rule I ended up with is to keep test-case slots at or below 0.75× the cores, with slots plus judge workers at or below cores minus 2. Under that rule one judge host sustained about 90 submissions per minute. A run at 145 per minute released wrong scores. An agentic training step with real per-submission feedback needs 190–240 per minute, which is the next capacity problem.

Timeouts can be queueing. 26 "judge timeouts" turned out to be submissions waiting in a stalled judge's queue; re-judged, they took 5 seconds in total. Waits and stalls are now logged separately, and the grading wait is long enough that queueing can't look like failure.

Cheap evals, chosen from data. All 172 problems × 5 samples cost about 9 GPU-hours; the 40-problem subset about 2, and it can be read off the full run for free. Packing 20 requests per eval job instead of 10 halved GPU-hours at the same wall time. The 1-hour agentic eval cap was set because 0 of 320 trials ran past 55 minutes; a 3-hour cap would triple the worst-case cost per checkpoint. On the 27B baselines, raising a 32K per-call output cap fixed a setting that truncated 37% of responses and wasted about 90% of output tokens.

Team RL: freezing a running team

A team-outcome reward can't say which fork or message helped. The tool I built for that freezes the whole team just before one decision, samples alternatives from the same state, runs each to the end, and credits the decision with the difference (a leave-one-out baseline, as in counterfactual credit-assignment work). On a spec-to-repo task, one fork decision led to 2, 1 or 151 passing tests out of 211, depending only on what was sampled at that point. One decision carries a large, learnable signal.

Freezing a team means capturing more than text. It includes every agent's context and pending request, each working tree and the container filesystem, the message queues (undelivered messages too), the whiteboard and the timers. The costs that matter:

  • Freezing is cheap. The snapshot takes about a second and restoring an agent to its first model call about five. Alternatives start as soon as the snapshot is committed, not when the original trial ends, which in one case was 550 s later.
  • Run alternatives in parallel, each on its own server. A decision then costs its slowest branch (374 s), not the sum (1,020 s), and a dedicated server per branch raised throughput 52%.
  • Route a whole team to one server. Branches share almost their entire prompt, so prefix-cache hits are 97–98% and computed prefill is 2–3% of prompt tokens. Random routing would have cost 57% more prefill.
  • Pick decisions carefully. Each branch still generated 130–145K tokens, and a late submit decision on an algorithm task gave three identical zeros: no signal for the cost.

Getting the measurement right took an audit. The first version sampled the original decision from the request built before the freeze and the alternatives from the one rebuilt after restoring, which had one extra resume message. Now every alternative, including the original, restarts from the snapshot and resends a byte-identical request.

05 · Learnings

Silent failures: what corrupts RL without crashing it

None of these crashed a run. Each one either fed the optimizer a wrong signal or would have turned a real result into noise.

1 · Normalising by a tiny standard deviation amplifies noise

GRPO divides each group's reward differences by their standard deviation. With partial-credit rewards, a group of nearly identical scores, 0.549 / 0.549 / 0.550 / 0.553, becomes advantages of −0.74 / −0.74 / −0.22 / +1.69: a full-strength gradient on what is basically timing noise. Dropping the division (A = R − mean, which works for 0/1 rewards) went too far the other way: the gradient norm was 0.022, 10–20× too small for 0–1 partial credit. The fix is a floor: A = (R − mean) / max(std, 0.05).

2 · Sequence-parallel gradients on a hybrid model

Qwen3.5 mixes full attention with Gated DeltaNet linear-attention layers. In my full training stack with Ulysses sequence parallelism, the forward pass matched plain data parallelism but the backward pass did not. On a 47K-token sequence, the gradient's cosine similarity with data-parallel training was 0.851 (0.787 in the linear-attention layers, 0.666 in the embeddings), against a run-to-run noise floor of 0.9999. A second check on a 67K-token rollout, through a different trainer code path, found the lm_head gradient off as well: cosine 0.682 at 1.39× the expected norm. A head-parallel implementation of the linear-attention layers brought the first check back to 0.99991. For the single-call arm I simply turned sequence parallelism off and offloaded optimizer state instead.

I haven't pinned down the cause. On my own hardware, a different GPU architecture from the original runs, I ran the linear-attention layers alone at 2 and 4 sequence-parallel ranks, on multi-chunk and packed sequences, with and without activation checkpointing. The parameter gradients matched a full-sequence reference to cosine 0.99998 or better, and a control that cut the cross-rank state moved the outputs at least 10× more than sequence parallelism did. So the problem is either in how the pieces are put together in the full stack or specific to the original hardware. Two lessons hold either way:

  • Log-prob parity checks only test the forward pass. Before trusting any parallelism scheme on a new architecture, compare its gradient with plain data parallelism on one real sequence, in the exact stack you train with.
  • Random initialisation hides this class of bug. With default initialisation these gates forget their state within a few tokens, so almost nothing crosses the rank boundary and a gradient test on random layers passes no matter what. Set the decay to trained-model-like values first.

3 · Optimizer state that appears on step 2

Adam's moment buffers don't exist until the first optimizer.step(). A long-context run that passes step 1 comfortably can therefore run out of memory at step 2. The fix is to load offloaded optimizer state only around the optimizer step, and with several updates per step, re-offload after each. The general lesson is to smoke-test with at least two updates at full sequence length.

4 · Judges that fail silently

After a sandbox crash, one judge kept answering, but it graded known-correct solutions as "Wrong Answer": score 0, status done. Retries can't catch that, because nothing looks like an error. I added three fixes:

  • A canary: two known solutions with known scores, graded every few minutes. Any deviation pauses training.
  • A rule that infrastructure failures are masked, never scored 0.
  • A cap on load per judge, since at around 144 concurrent submissions a judge stalled while clients waited up to an hour.

5 · One token over

A trial died three times with "maximum context length is 262144 tokens; you requested … for a total of at least 262145". In every case the request was exactly one token over. The harness estimated prompt size at about four characters per token, but one tool result was 22K characters of numbers, and this tokenizer emits one token per digit. The 13K-token underestimate exceeded the harness's safety margin. The server then clamped the output budget to the space left but counted one more prompt token in a later check, so the request failed by exactly one token, and failed again the same way on every resume.

6 · Red-team the grader before the policy does

Every grader I built or adopted got an adversarial audit before it was allowed to produce reward. Every audit found something. No policy found these first, but an RL policy optimising against the grader eventually would.

06 · Process

Running an RL campaign with a team of AI agents

The work itself was done collectively. A coordinator agent and a rotating set of builder, auditor and red-team agents, all running in Claude Code, wrote the harness, ran the experiments and reported back while I made the calls. That's a small preview of the subject, and it taught me a few rules I now apply to every run:

  • A validity table on every check-in. KL, entropy, gradient norm, the trainer-vs-inference log-prob gap, truncation rate, the fraction of groups with any learning signal, speculative-decoding acceptance, prefix-cache hit rate, degenerate outputs, and the judge canary. A green dashboard has to be green on all of them.
  • Pre-flight from artifacts, not summaries. The checklist is checked against the actual files and logs, not against an agent's description of them. That is how the all-zero 16K run was caught in pre-flight, before it burned real compute.
  • Speed first, then go. Push serving and training speed until the next improvement is under about 5%, without changing the recipe, then launch.
  • No pointless ablations. Use one best-known recipe per arm. The first steps of the real run are the pre-flight.
  • Red-team every build, then read the diff. Every audit found a real blocker, and the grader exploits in §5 were all caught this way.
  • Recount every claim. The coordinator withdrew or corrected about fifteen of its own claims over four days after recounting against data, including a KL diagnosis, an overstated drop rate, and the expected benefit of a design change. That's the system working, not failing.
  • Read trajectories. The 27-token thinking and the self-deleted solutions in §3 came from reading, not from metrics.
  • Agents stall quietly. Background agents can sit for hours on a prompt waiting for approval. Give them scripts instead of long inline commands, and watch when their output files last changed.

07 · Where this is going

The collective-intelligence idea, briefly

Plan, not a result

Nothing in this section has been trained or measured yet. It explains why the baselines above were run and what the next experiments test.

The question is whether post-training can teach one shared policy to work as a team: when to fork a copy of itself, what to share and with whom, whose result to trust, and when the team is done. Three things set this apart from simply sampling more:

  • The harness has no organisation chart. Any agent can fork a child that inherits its full context and an exact copy of its working tree. Agents message each other directly, share an append-only whiteboard, can read each other's recent actions, and any agent can submit. These are the primitives behind the "collective" columns in Table 1.
  • One reward for the whole team: the task's own verifier score. Forking, messaging and agreeing are never rewarded directly, because that would teach ceremonial communication and let plausible errors spread.
  • The control decides the claim. The same starting checkpoint is trained two ways, as a solo agent and as a team, with matched training tokens. Both are then evaluated solo, as independent parallel samples with a selector at the end, and as an online team. A team-specific gain only counts if team training improves the online team more than solo training does, beyond what extra parallel samples give.

Credit assignment is the hard part: one team reward says little about which fork or message helped. The freeze-and-branch machinery in §4 exists for that, and so far it is a measured tool, not a learning result.

What comes next

  1. Agentic solo arm, rebuilt. The minimal loop is red-teamed and its eval is running on the base model and the step-40 checkpoint. RL starts once those are in, with a go/no-go rule set in advance: by step 10, reward on revisited training tasks must be rising.
  2. Single-call restart from step 40, with an entropy-preserving change that actually acts when there's one update per step, and a larger problem pool.
  3. A short team-RL pilot, roughly 5–10% of the full budget, with paired solo-vs-team evals at every checkpoint and freeze-and-branch credit on fork, message and submit decisions.
  4. Several attempts per task on every headline comparison. Table 1 shows why one isn't enough.

Notes

How the numbers were made

The task is the statistical unit. Comparisons are paired over the same problems and seeds, with task-level bootstrap intervals (2,000 draws) or sign tests. Each arm is evaluated in the mode it trains in and compared with its own base model. Eval sets are excluded from training, curriculum selection and checkpoint selection. Infrastructure failures are masked, never scored as zero. The 27B baselines are single attempts and descriptive only.
