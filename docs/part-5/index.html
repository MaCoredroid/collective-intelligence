When the Reward Misses the Algorithm

Adding an LLM judge to verifiable reinforcement learning

I want a small coding model to acquire useful optimisation algorithms, including simulated annealing, and improve its FrontierCS performance. A precisely computed reward may still leave that capability weakly supervised: strong greedy code can beat fragile search, and a finite score does not identify how a program works. I tried adding an LLM judge to the executable reward for one-shot training, using code-grounded judgments to shape credit. The early checklist judge often agreed with itself while barely changing the update. Richer rankings supplied a stronger signal, whose value needs a separate test. Benchmark scores rose before that stronger intervention; that rise cannot establish its benefit. A fresh-instance audit and a search-headroom probe make the remaining question concrete: does semantic judgment encourage better algorithms, or another appearance of progress?

  2. Coverage and influence are different. One update ranked 15 of 16 kept groups, yet the reshaping displaced only 0.05% of absolute advantage mass.
  3. A clock read was a misleading search signal. The inspected clocks seeded random generators. Fixed-iteration local search existed and must be counted separately.
  4. The judge needs an independent outcome test. Its first fresh-instance disagreement audit reached just three pairs from one task.
  5. The target skill had useful headroom. A separate coding-agent probe improved over the policy’s best available programs on 16 of 20 selected training tasks. This was not a judge-training result.
Experiment snapshot · preliminary evidence

This article combines saved experiment readouts with inspection of the training objective. Protocols changed during the run. I distinguish the judge’s intended role, its measured update influence and its unproven causal benefit. The fresh-instance audit is incomplete; later matched checkpoint evaluations were still pending at this snapshot. The figures include explicitly labelled mechanism illustrations.

01 · The objective gap

What did the higher score teach?

A higher test score leaves a second question open: what capability did the policy actually acquire? I wanted an agent that could write a working search, then improve it through feedback. A score on a set of optimisation instances tells me how its program performed there. It does not, by itself, tell me whether the program used simulated annealing, a sharper greedy construction, a fixed-work local search, or an assumption that will fail on a different input.

That distinction mattered in Part 3. Search was rare and often less reliable than the student’s greedy code. An outcome objective could favour the competence the model already had. In Part 4, I tried to borrow a foothold from a 27B teacher through long agent traces. The student learned a way of spending time more readily than a reliable search algorithm. I proposed separating construction from feedback-driven improvement.

The next experiments returned to the first stage: produce one complete program in a single response, with thinking before the code. That removed the tool-using episode, but it did not automatically make the teacher easy to imitate. The first one-shot SFT set still had a median response near 89K tokens. Its clean development read regressed sharply, and an OPD continuation drifted further toward the length cap. One response can still contain a very long dependency.

The short-demonstration bridge from Part 4

Reducing the teacher’s reasoning effort shortened its responses but also reduced its search. A generic teacher-only search hint at medium effort supplied shorter, more search-oriented demonstrations. The student did not receive that hint. A filtered set of 459 demonstrations covered 130 problems and produced a promising development read. Length, prompting and data selection changed together, so this is not a controlled demonstration that shorter reasoning alone fixed transfer.

That development read is not a held-out capability verdict. The later hybrid RL arm discussed here started from the earlier single-call RL checkpoint, rather than forming a clean SFT → OPD → hybrid-RL chain from that development result. I keep those lineages separate.

Analysis · A correct measurement can leave a weak teaching signal

The verifier’s calculation can be entirely correct while its task distribution gives little advantage to the behaviour I want. If a sound search wins only on larger or different instances, the current cases may not reveal that benefit. If the student’s attempted search is simply broken, the low score is appropriate. Those possibilities demand different responses: improve coverage in the first case, acquire the missing construction in the second.

I also need to separate this from reward hacking. A valid greedy solver that performs well is not cheating. Copying a baseline may be a rational response to a weak policy and the available reward. A claim of exploitation needs a specific loophole and evidence that the intended objective failed. The more modest problem is already enough to motivate another signal: the measured outcome may not distinguish the kind of progress I am trying to teach.

02 · A second signal

Ask about the implemented program

I added an LLM judge to inspect the task and candidate programs. The intended contribution was a semantic judgment: does the implementation contain a substantive improvement, does its claimed search affect the returned solution, and are there obvious brittle assumptions or broken mechanisms that the sampled score leaves unclear?

The judge saw anonymous programs without their verifier scores. That matters because copying the observed score order would merely repackage the existing reward. The role was to add information from the code. Compilation, actual execution and trustworthy validity facts remained with the host verifier. A judge’s claim that a program is hardcoded was not sufficient evidence to authenticate that accusation or alter an executable result.

The earlier checklist asked about baseline copying, hardcoding to tests, whether an improvement loop existed and changed the output, and failures on independently executed shared tests. The later quality-ranking version retained those checks and asked for relative judgments among programs. Each group was shown in two orders, with agreement rules before rankings could affect training.

The criteria had to remain method-neutral. A strong direct, constructive or greedy solution should beat a weak annealer. Merely mentioning “simulated annealing,” adding a dead loop or writing more complex code should not earn credit. The capability I want is a useful algorithm, not membership in a prestigious algorithm family.

Analysis · Semantic supervision is a hypothesis about future performance

Some of these properties can be tested directly: compare an incremental score against full recomputation, instrument a loop, check feasibility and termination, or execute on fresh valid instances. I am not claiming that unit tests can never capture them. The practical gap is what the current reward captures, at the current cost and coverage. The judge can suggest distinctions worth testing before I have a complete executable specification for every one.

That makes the judge another proxy. It may confuse familiar code with sound code, favour confident explanations, or penalise an unconventional but correct method. Anonymous presentation and two-order agreement check consistency; they do not establish truth. The experiment needs to show that the distinctions the judge introduces predict useful outcomes the original score missed.

03 · The learning rule

How judgment entered the update

The recipe evolved. An early design combined verifier reward and judge preferences directly. The later version used the judge to reshape group-relative training advantages among eligible student programs. Those are different interventions. A fixed mixture coefficient from an early plan is not an accurate description of the later run.

For the later reshaping component, take the outcome-based advantages after the host’s validity and masking rules. The judge supplies quality tiers. Within the eligible positive advantages, multiply by tier factors and renormalize to preserve their total. Do the analogous operation separately for negative advantages, using inverse factors so that a better-ranked negative candidate receives a smaller penalty.

For Aᵢ > 0: A′ᵢ = Aᵢ fᵢ · (Σⱼ Aⱼ) / (Σⱼ Aⱼ fⱼ) j ranges over eligible positive advantages

In the pinned stronger contract, positive tier factors range from 1 to 0.1. Negative factors use the inverse ordering, with their own normalization. The point is to change the allocation of learning credit while preserving the positive and negative totals separately. This conservation applies after host masking and only to the reshaping component. Separate teacher, repair and non-baseline channels are not included in that statement.

Analysis · It can redirect reinforcement without creating every missing lesson

A highly ranked candidate can receive more positive credit than before. A promising candidate with a negative advantage can be penalized less. But this component preserves its sign: a negative advantage does not become positive, and zero stays zero. When the entire base-advantage group is flat, there is no signal for this component to redistribute, even if the judge has a strong preference.

This is a useful limit to make visible. The judge can influence which already sampled behaviours are reinforced most strongly; the rule does not guarantee a foothold for a capability the student never produces. A separate additive signal would have different power and different exposure to judge error. The curriculum question therefore survives: does the policy sample enough executable constructions for this feedback to act on?

The source also supports judge-proposed extra inputs that are checked against task constraints and executed through the real verifier. Turning a semantic suspicion into a reproducible test is particularly attractive. I do not have usage evidence sufficient to credit that path with the run’s improvement, so it remains a supported mechanism rather than a measured explanation.

04 · Check the signal

The judge must reach the loss

Calling an experiment “verifier plus judge” is not enough. First I need to establish that the judge’s answers were accepted in time, distinguished the candidates and changed the learning signal. Otherwise an apparent hybrid run may be behaving almost like the outcome-only recipe.

One update made the distinction unusually clear. It accepted a ranking for 15 of 16 kept groups, yet the reshaping’s absolute advantage displacement was just 0.05% of final absolute advantage mass. Many candidates received identical checklist flags. The judge could agree consistently and still provide almost no relative guidance within a group.

Earlier updates had another failure mode: judgments were rejected or arrived after the update deadline, leaving little effective coverage. That is an implementation and measurement lesson, not evidence against semantic supervision. Once coverage improved, the checklist’s inability to distinguish candidates became visible. The later protocol added score-blind quality ranking beyond the checklist.

By update 63, all 16 kept groups had accepted rankings, and the same displacement diagnostic was 11.8%. That establishes a more substantial intervention in the recorded advantages. It does not establish that the judge was correct or that the resulting parameter update improved search.

Analysis · Consistency, influence and value are three different gates

The first gate is whether repeated presentations produce a usable judgment. The second is whether that judgment changes the learning signal. The third is whether that change helps on an independent target. Passing one gate does not imply the next. A high agreement rate can conceal uniform tiers; a high displacement rate can amplify a mistaken preference.

The recorded share is also easy to overread. It measures Σ|A′−A| divided by Σ|A′|, not the fraction of the parameter gradient attributable to the judge. Candidate sequence lengths, token likelihoods and optimizer state intervene between an advantage and an update. The plots describe a changing run with changing task draws, not an ablation isolating the ranking protocol.

05 · Keep the readouts separate

The score rose before the stronger judge

The one-shot FrontierCS read improved in absolute score. On 172 problems with five attempts per problem, Avg@5 was 12.56 for the historical start, 13.72 at checkpoint 24 and 14.54 at checkpoint 50. Avg@5 is the mean score across those attempts and problems, not pass@5 and not the best of five.

Analysis · Neither a score trend nor a behaviour tag is an attribution

The result is worth recording, with its limits. Compilation improved. Best@5 fell between checkpoints 24 and 50 while the mean rose. Forced or truncated responses also became more common. These are different aspects of the policy; no single number establishes that it acquired the targeted search capability.

Nor does this sequence isolate the judge from outcome learning or the other changes in the recipe. A matched verifier-only control would be needed for that attribution. The later matched checkpoint read, following the stronger ranking protocol, was still pending at the source snapshot. I do not fill that gap with the already available checkpoint-50 score.

06 · Read the program

The clock was only a seed

An apparent search signal exposed a second measurement problem. Some programs read a clock, and a broad heuristic treated them as time-bounded search. A stricter inspection found that the clock reads seeded random-number generators. That is a use of randomness, but it does not establish a loop that keeps improving a solution until its time budget expires.

The stricter read covered 960 training programs from updates 55–59 and 860 answers at each of checkpoints 24 and 50. It found no clock-budget-controlled loop in those sets, including a broader check for a clock-based break. Fixed-work local search did exist: one inspected program performed a fixed million-swap pass. “No timed loop” is therefore not equivalent to “no search,” and it is certainly not a proof that the model cannot learn search.

The same audit found no detectable judge preference or penalty for local-search programs against similar-score alternatives. On the selected search-pays tasks the estimated rank gap was +0.094 with an interval [−0.341, +0.500], from 53 pairs in 16 groups. That is not evidence that the judge suppressed search, nor strong evidence that it encouraged it.

Analysis · A semantic reward still needs a definition of the behaviour

This is where the judge can be useful and where it needs scrutiny. It should inspect reachable operations, the score update, the acceptance rule and the connection to the returned result. A keyword or a timer is an inexpensive proxy. Reading code adds semantic information, but a judge can still be fooled by a plausible scaffold whose useful path never runs.

I now separate greedy construction, fixed-work local search, time-budgeted search and working annealing. They are related but different categories. The final check should execute the claimed mechanism and test its invariants. This is artifact-level supervision about code quality; it should not be described as step-level supervision of the model’s reasoning unless those intermediate labels are actually present.

07 · Judge the judge

Disagreement is where the hypothesis gets tested

A judge that disagrees with the observed verifier score might see something valuable: a general algorithm that suffered on the sampled cases, a brittle higher-scoring alternative, or an implementation that should scale better. It might also be wrong. Disagreement is the important audit cohort because repeating the existing score order adds little evidence of complementary information.

I selected pairs where the host-accepted judge preferred a program whose normalized verifier score was at least 0.05 lower. The test is to run both programs on fresh, judge-unseen inputs under the same execution rules. New sizes are useful only where the task’s stated domain permits them. The judge must not become the authority that certifies its own preference.

Across updates 56–63, the judge agreed with the verifier’s order on 52.4–66.3% of clear-gap pairs per update. Ties accounted for another 7.1–33.3%. Those ties are not reversals. Nor is disagreement itself a failure: the reason for adding the judge was to supply information beyond the current score. The useful question is whether its departures from that score predict something independently valuable.

The first round found 296 disagreement pairs across the recorded updates, but available official generators allowed only three primary pairs from one task to be tested. All three judge picks lost. That is evidence about those programs on those fresh cases. It is too narrow to determine whether the judge generally sees useful quality or merely rewards appearance.

The controls were more encouraging as an execution check: 44 of 49 pairs preserved the jointly preferred program’s win, with five ties and no losses. But they cover only three tasks and a different selected cohort. Their rate cannot repair the disagreement cohort’s missing coverage or be treated as a matched estimate of the effect of disagreement.

The recorded baseline-copy flags also remained common, ranging from 17.9% to 31.5% of judged programs across those updates. Those are model-assigned flags, not independently verified exploits. They show why a rubric’s intention is not an outcome: placing copies low in a ranking did not by itself establish that the policy stopped producing them.

Analysis · Missing coverage is part of the result

Most candidate tasks lacked a released instance generator. A broader second round was being built with constraint-checked inputs. Such generators need their own validation: legal inputs and correct checker handling, plausible coverage of the task domain, and separation from the judge that made the ranking. Passing a checker with a baseline program is useful evidence, not a guarantee of representative coverage.

When that read is complete, I want wins, ties, losses and score margins, with uncertainty grouped by task. Many correlated pairs from one problem must not masquerade as many independent problems. I also want failure cases inspected directly: did the judge favour a better general mechanism, or a familiar algorithm name over a stronger implementation?

Independent evaluation matters because both reward channels can be gamed. Zheng et al. document position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases in LLM evaluation. In a different mathematical setting, Kenton et al. observe policies exploiting an AI judge and study debate as a mitigation. They also report a smaller train–validation reward gap with LLM judging than with verifiable reward in their setting. That supports investigating complementary judgment, not a universal ordering of which reward is safer.

08 · Check the target

Could useful search beat the policy here?

The last question is whether the intended capability has room to help on the training tasks. A stronger preference for search would be misguided if sound search could not improve the objective. I commissioned a separate coding-agent probe to build time-budgeted annealing or local-search programs for 20 selected tasks from the search-pays pool.

The tasks were selected by repeated occurrence in recent updates, with a decoded statement and native grader available, rather than by observed headroom. The probe’s programs scored an average of 58.33, against 51.63 for the policy’s best available whole program per task and 31.36 for its mean. The policy comparison used 520 scored samples across those tasks from updates 56–63, including compile failures and truncated outputs.

The gap against the policy’s best was +6.70 points [3.61, 9.95]. The probe won on 16 of 20 tasks, tied on one and lost on three. Removing one task affected by a checker issue produced a similar estimate. This supplies concrete evidence that useful algorithmic headroom existed on the selected cases.

Analysis · Headroom is a target, not proof of transfer

A separate coding agent with iterative authoring is not the same policy operating under the same generation budget. The probe also used existing training cases, not new held-out problems. The programs differed in more than the presence of a timed loop. I cannot attribute the entire gain to annealing, claim a fair model comparison, or treat it as evidence that the LLM judge taught the student that capability.

The student-facing wrapper asked for a competitive-programming solution in C++ and only code. Scoring objectives lived inside individual statements, while a separate prompt audit found missing time limits in many evaluation prompts. That is a concrete specification issue to inspect. It does not establish that changing the prompt would create the skill. The gap could involve instruction framing, constructions the policy rarely samples, or the training signal acting on those constructions.

The result does sharpen the next comparison. If the target programs exist and improve the actual objective, does verifier-plus-judge training make such working constructions more frequent than matched verifier-only training? Does that improvement survive fresh inputs? A higher judge score is not sufficient evidence for either question.

09 · The next conclusion

Two proxies, one independent target

The attempt has already changed how I think about supervision. A verifiable reward is precise about the test it implements. An LLM judge can express distinctions the current test leaves implicit. Its flexibility can supply a useful inductive bias, but it also introduces another surface for mistakes and optimization.

The immediate lesson is to measure all three links: whether the judge distinguishes the programs, whether those distinctions reach the update, and whether the resulting policy improves on an independent target. The present record establishes the first two in parts of the run. It does not yet establish a causal benefit from the stronger judge, or a reduction in reward hacking.

That returns me to the curriculum from Part 4. First acquire enough correct algorithmic constructions to give feedback something to work with. Then train improvement explicitly, using executable outcomes and carefully validated semantic guidance. Finally ask whether those skills compose across a longer agent episode.

The unresolved question is now narrower than “can an LLM judge replace the reward?” I want to know whether it adds useful information about algorithmic progress—and whether the student can turn that information into programs that work beyond the cases that trained it.

Notes

How to read this experiment

This is a one-shot coding study on a 9B policy, with FrontierSmith training tasks and FCS172 evaluation. Later reshaping used groups of eight student programs. Checkpoints 24 and 50 share the strict response packet: about 96K thinking tokens plus 32K answer tokens, with injected-control accounting inside the total response budget. The historical start has the answer-cap mismatch noted above. Avg@5 averages five attempts per task; Best@5 averages each task’s maximum. Neither is algorithmic pass@k.

Intervals are 95% and come from saved reports; no training or grading computation was rerun to author this article. The checkpoint comparisons use paired per-problem bootstraps. The headroom probe uses paired task gaps on a selected training subset. The fresh-instance judge cohort was too narrow for a population-level conclusion. Code inspection supports the reshaping mechanism; deployment and influence are reported separately. Source snapshot: October 23, 2026, approximately 12:50 Pacific. Later results are not implied.

Research context: Zheng et al., Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena; Kenton et al., Debate Training Reduces Reward Hacking in RLAIF; Lightman et al., Let’s Verify Step by Step. The latter studies intermediate-step supervision; the code judgments here are not a replication of that method. Off-policy teacher guidance and diversity were separate motivations in this evolving recipe: LUFFY and DARLING. Their results do not establish the effectiveness of this combined coding recipe.
