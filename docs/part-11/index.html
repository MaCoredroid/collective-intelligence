Demystifying On-Policy Distillation

How a 9B coding model learned from its future self

A 9B coding model reached its teacher’s FrontierCS score range in ten on-policy distillation updates. Both started from the same released weights. The teacher took 124 reinforcement learning updates and roughly 747 million trainer tokens to get there. We look at what the student gained from its future self and how that run compares with our earlier attempts using a larger teacher.

What we trained

In Part 9, we changed the temperature, the token-level credit, and the mix of teacher feedback and execution rewards. Our best OPD result came when we changed who taught the student. We took a later, RL-trained version of the same 9B, froze it, and asked it to score attempts written by its earlier self.

In on-policy distillation (OPD), the student writes the attempt. The teacher scores each sampled token given what the student has written so far. The update makes tokens more likely when the teacher favors them relative to the student. An execution judge grades the finished program separately. This creates a practical question for every recipe. Can the teacher recognize useful next moves along the paths the student actually takes?

  1. Flash SFT → Flash OPD trained on fixed teacher demonstrations, then used Flash to score the student’s own attempts. Average score improved after OPD, and best-of-five barely moved.
  2. OPD from the 9B’s future self used the released 9B’s own attempts and an RL-trained version of the same model as the teacher. Ten OPD updates brought the student into the score range the teacher reached after 124 RL updates.
  3. Teacher-guided RL trained the RL 9B on hard problems with hints drawn from 27B solutions. The pilot showed no clear gain when tested without hints.
  4. Full SFT from the pretrained 9B used graded teacher demonstrations. More epochs recovered score and widened coverage. We then tried a refreshed demonstration mix.

What we tried with the larger teacher

We started from Qwen’s released, post-trained 9B and ran 207 SFT updates over three epochs on 1,110 Flash demonstrations from 268 tasks. We filtered incomplete answer code and used the student’s starting loss to screen out some hard-to-imitate answers. The main OPD comparisons then ran for five full-weight updates at a learning rate of 10−6, using attempts generated by that SFT checkpoint.

  • Raw teacher probabilities. Using Flash at temperature 1.0 cut the one-attempt mean from 7.58 to 3.64. Adding outcome reward raised it to 4.43.
  • Temperature calibration. On the same starting student prefixes, Flash’s next-token probabilities were more spread out. Scoring at 0.8 brought the two entropies closer and recovered much of the early score drop. This measured the spread of the distributions. It left open whether the two models favored the same tokens.
  • Discounted credit. Letting later teacher scores affect earlier updates raised Avg@5 from 6.88 to 8.86. Best@5 barely moved, coverage stayed at 86 of 172 tasks, and forced thinking closes rose from 24% to 57%.
  • Longer runs with execution rewards. Combining OPD and execution rewards in the same updates, and alternating them in separate blocks, peaked at 9.21 and 9.18 Avg@5 respectively. Alternating made the stopping behavior move with the objective, while the benchmark gain over the five-update run remained small.

Part 9 · What Did Distillation Buy?

Distilling from the 9B’s future self

The student started from Qwen’s released, post-trained Qwen3.5-9B. The teacher started from the same release and had completed 124 outcome-based RL updates on judged coding attempts across its training runs. We froze that teacher and skipped the supervised stage for the student.

We sampled fresh one-shot coding attempts from the student and used the teacher to score the sampled reasoning and code tokens. Each update targeted 64 student rollouts. The run kept the teacher scoring temperature at 0.8 and used rank-64 LoRA adapters. The final recipe used ten updates at 4 × 10−5. The execution judge graded the final programs.

What changed between the first and final LoRA runs

The rollout server generated with the adapter merged into the model weights and rounded to BF16. The trainer added the adapter contribution in higher precision during its forward pass. The trainer and server could therefore assign different probabilities to the same token, even with the same adapter loaded. Those probabilities matter because the update uses tokens generated by the deployed student.

We changed the trainer’s forward pass to use the rounded, merged weights while retaining gradients for the adapter. We kept the original starting weights for the reference KL. To check a reload, we scored the same sampled tokens immediately before and after the rollout server loaded the new weights and compared both against the trainer.

The first LoRA run also barely moved from its starting policy at a learning rate of 10−5. We increased it to 4 × 10−5 and ran past the earlier stopping point. The KL from the starting policy at the second batch rose from 8.4 × 10−5 to 2.8 × 10−3. The probability probe verified closer agreement after the sync fix. The benchmark result reflects the revised recipe as a whole.

Evaluation after ten OPD updates

We evaluated the step-10 OPD checkpoint on 172 FrontierCS tasks, with five attempts per task. The result comes from the checkpoint exported immediately after OPD, before the two subsequent outcome-RL updates.

The student scored 14.03 Avg@5 and its teacher 13.91. The task-paired difference was +0.12 points, with a 95% interval of −1.72 to +2.00. The first attempt alone scored 15.39, and the other four averaged 13.69. Best@5 was 1.06 points higher and coverage differed by one task. The five-attempt evaluation puts the student in the teacher’s score range.

This was the best OPD result we found. It scored 5.17 Avg@5 points above the earlier Flash-distilled student, with a paired interval of +2.91 to +7.69. The teacher, student initialization, trainable parameters, learning rate, and number of updates all changed. We prepared a comparison using Flash with the same released student, adapter recipe, and task draws. That comparison was still pending at the November 14 evidence cutoff.

What it took to reach the teacher’s score

The teacher’s 124 outcome-RL updates used about 747 million trainer tokens. The logs count 636 million across the final 84 updates, and we estimate another 111 million for the first 40 from the number and mean length of the training rollouts. The student reached the same FrontierCS score range after ten OPD updates. The first five updates logged 17.9 million student response tokens for distillation. We have that count for half of the student run, so the full ten-update token total is still open. Part 9 · Training budget under outcome RL

The ten updates measure how quickly the released 9B caught up once a trained teacher was available. The marginal compute for OPD includes generating the student’s attempts, scoring them with the frozen teacher, and updating the student. Starting from scratch also requires the RL that produced the teacher.

Where the score gain came from

Qwen’s released 9B scored 6.26 Avg@5 in an earlier evaluation on the same tasks with a different launcher, deadline, and judging route. We used its first two attempts to group tasks by score and reserved its other three attempts to estimate the baseline within each group. Even a model that missed both grouping attempts can score on its next try.

The 27 tasks where the released 9B scored highest in its first two attempts supplied just over half the measured gain. On the 117 tasks that scored zero on both grouping attempts, the distilled student earned credit on 52, the teacher on 54, and the released 9B on 23 in its three reserved attempts. These counts describe performance on a low-scoring group with different numbers of attempts for the compared policies.

We inspected the best programs on 14 deliberately selected tasks. Several gains over the earlier Flash run came from compiling or staying within the time limit. Against the RL teacher, the cases went both ways. The student’s alternative graph construction scored higher on one task. On another, its unbounded query strategy failed an adversarial test that the teacher’s bounded version handled.

What does it mean for a teacher to be close?

OPD sees the prefixes the student generates. If a useful reasoning or coding move is rarely sampled, the teacher rarely gets to directly reinforce it. Starting from the same weights and tokenizer can help the two models agree on how to continue a prefix. Training the teacher with verified rewards on the target domain can also make its feedback more useful. In our best run, the models shared a starting checkpoint and the teacher had learned from graded coding attempts. We did not measure how much each factor contributed.

Teacher-trace SFT is one way to prepare a student for a more distant teacher. In Rethinking On-Policy Distillation, the authors used full-weight SFT to train Qwen3-1.7B-Base on about 200,000 responses from the same Qwen3-4B teacher they used for OPD. After warm-up, their measured overlap in next-token probability mass was higher and more stable on student-generated prefixes. Rethinking On-Policy Distillation

The amount of SFT matters. A controlled study of Qwen models found that a brief warm-up on verified teacher solutions improved later OPD, with diminishing returns after the first validation-loss plateau. The same study found that RL could improve the student’s initial task score while making it less compatible with a particular teacher. This makes probability overlap on the student’s own prefixes a more direct diagnostic than parameter count or pre-OPD benchmark score. Understanding the Synergy between SFT, RLVR, and OPD

A separate math study, Train Ahead, Distill Back, also used a later policy to teach an earlier one. Its future teacher came from self-distillation and could see verified solution context. Our coding teacher came from outcome RL and scored the student’s own prefixes. The two results motivate testing how much a stronger teacher can transfer when it shares the student’s starting point. Teacher-guided RL with 27B hints

We ran a separate 20-update pilot from the same outcome-trained 9B that served as the OPD teacher. Inspired by the Monotone Frontier Curriculum, we used graded Qwen3.8-27B solutions to supply a sketch, partial code, or a full program on hard training problems. The 9B generated its own answer and received the execution score. The curriculum included attempts without a hint and reduced the amount of help when enough attempts succeeded. Monotone Frontier Curriculum paper

At step 20, with hints removed, the 9B scored 13.02 on a one-attempt FrontierCS evaluation versus 14.39 for its starting checkpoint. The paired difference was −1.37 points with a 95% interval of −3.99 to +1.30. A trace audit found no use of the 27B’s core idea in 720 unhinted training attempts. Most hinted wins depended on access to the full program, which the student often copied. We stopped the pilot after 20 updates. A matched plain-RL control was left unrun.

Part 10 · Flash-to-9B handoffs with a frozen policy

Full supervised fine-tuning from the pretrained base

We wanted to know whether demonstrations from stronger models could give outcome RL a better starting policy. Earlier offline SFT on the released, post-trained weights had sometimes regressed. This run put the demonstrations at the start of our post-training pipeline, using Qwen3.5-9B-Base weights with the released post-trained tokenizer and chat template.

Data and training

We assembled 1,905 graded one-shot demonstrations on 274 training problems, with about 105 million retained assistant tokens per pass. The mix included 690 Qwen3.8-27B traces, 1,119 Flash traces, and 96 archived GLM traces. We kept FrontierCS evaluation tasks out of the training mix. We updated all model weights and applied equal loss weight to reasoning and answer tokens. The main evaluation used the epoch-three checkpoint after 357 updates and a continuation on the same data through epoch eight.

Evaluation against the raw base

We evaluated the raw base weights with the same tokenizer, template, and evaluation path. The base averaged 7.99 and closed thinking naturally on about 89% of attempts. After three passes over the demonstrations, Avg@5 was 2.62 points lower, with a task-paired 95% interval of −4.88 to −0.56.

After eight passes, SFT earned credit on 15 more tasks than the base and reduced compile errors from about 37% to 24%. Its average score remained 1.30 points below base, with a 95% interval of −3.45 to +0.71. The model produced a scoring answer on more tasks across five tries. The expected score of a single attempt had yet to improve over the base.

A separate one-attempt audit found that compilation failures on tasks the base answered successfully accounted for much of the epoch-three score loss. The SFT policy also ended reasoning naturally less often. The one-attempt screens moved from 0.72 at epoch one to 5.05 at epoch three, dipped to 3.44 at epoch five, and reached 7.04 at epoch eight. We treated undertraining as a working explanation for the recovery and tracked compilation separately from stopping.

What changed in the next SFT round

We chose Flash for the new batch after it scored higher on 132 shared FrontierSmith problems and yielded about 1.6 times as many retained tokens per GPU-hour under the settings we tested. We added 1,577 new Flash traces after screening for incomplete code and dropped 82 older 27B traces whose reasoning had been forcibly closed. The refreshed mix contained 3,400 traces and about 147.5 million retained tokens per pass.

We resumed from epoch seven of the original mix and ran 426 updates over two passes. The one-attempt score fell to 2.54 after the first pass and recovered to 7.27 after the second, close to 7.04 after epoch eight of the original mix. The paired interval for that final difference was −3.05 to +3.48.

The data and learning-rate schedule changed together, so the first-pass dip had more than one possible cause. A third round started from the refreshed mix’s second-pass checkpoint and trained for four epochs on the 1,577 new Flash traces alone. The refreshed mix’s five-attempt results and the third round’s evaluation were still pending at the November 14 evidence cutoff.

What we learned

The strongest OPD run used the 9B’s future self and reached its teacher’s score range without a teacher-trace warm-up on the student. An otherwise matched Flash run would test how much of the result came from using a teacher with the same starting weights. The guided RL pilot answered a different question. The student could use supplied hints during training, with no clear gain once they were removed.

The base-model SFT runs test whether demonstrations can prepare a better starting policy for outcome RL. By epoch eight, coverage was wider and average score remained below the untreated base point estimate. The next test is to give the raw base and a selected SFT checkpoint the same outcome-RL tasks and budget, then compare held-out score, coverage, compilation, and stopping. The full budget also includes teacher generation and SFT.
