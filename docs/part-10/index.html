Where Agent Teams Get Stuck

Training a 9B coding team to build, test, and choose a solution

A 9B coding team trained on Flash demonstrations submitted zero solutions in 197 FrontierCS rollouts. Training on tool calls and messages brought back submissions in a small follow-up. In handoff experiments, the 9B gained little from inheriting a plan or a first build. Scores rose most when Flash left behind competing programs with measured results. Teacher critiques prompted more changes of approach, with no clear score advantage over the controls.

197 attempts ended before the team submitted

Part 8 described a team harness that gives models the same basic tools. An agent can fork a teammate, exchange measurements, and submit one committed program for the team. The obvious next question was whether a smaller model could learn to use those tools well enough to combine what its teammates found.

We trained a 9B on 631 valid Flash team episodes. These provided 2,322 per-agent trajectories, about 158 million assistant tokens, and about 245 million sequence tokens once the surrounding context was included. We kept the small probe panel and our FrontierCS evaluation set out of SFT. The idea was to give the student practice following a full team episode before asking reinforcement learning to refine its choices.

The first rollouts from that checkpoint surprised us. None of 197 episodes submitted a solution. In the sampled run, 27% of turns lacked a tool call and the model produced about nine malformed calls per episode. The first RL update recorded zero loss and zero gradient. All the observed rewards were zero, which gave the group-relative outcome term no preference among the sampled attempts.

The output failures mattered in their own right. Much of the missing tool use came from turns that never closed their thinking block. A team that has found promising code can still end with no submitted solution if it cannot emit the final tool call. Our direct-RL team had used the interface successfully. That narrowed the question to what changed with the demonstrations and the checkpoint, while leaving several possible causes on the table.

Change the clock and the right moment to submit changes

The core interface has three moves. Fork starts a peer from the parent’s conversation and workspace snapshot, with its own seat identity and branch. Message passes an idea, test result or commit reference to a peer. Submit selects one committed program for the whole team. A peer can win the team’s submission without ever invoking the submit tool itself.

The original Flash traces paced work against elapsed time. Later we gave each 9B agent 100K generated tokens. A wall clock measures how much time a team occupies. A token clock controls the amount each agent can write, even when the models run at different speeds. More agents can still spend more tokens in aggregate, so comparing teams also calls for a total-work measure.

On the newer interface, the first submit_final call shows the live team state without submitting. A second call with confirm: true can select the exact commit and end the episode. The prompt asks agents to report measured results near the merge point and agree to a commit explicitly. Confirmation gives the submitter a chance to reconsider, but the tool does not require unanimous consent. That distinction later mattered: the 9B could satisfy the tool contract while skipping the comparison the prompt intended.

The old demonstrations contained successful endings under a different routine. In the initial audit, nearly half of turns mentioned time and about 9% were flagged for wall-clock behavior. Those flags are broad and can overlap. Replacing “two minutes remaining” with “tokens remaining” would leave intact the decisions that came before and after it. We chose to rerun Flash so the demonstrations could contain the choices it actually made after seeing the student’s budget and confirmation state.

Reinforcement learning changed how teams searched

Before the SFT run, we trained the 9B directly in the team harness. The first pilot assigned credit primarily from the verified team outcome, with a selection term on episodes where the graded candidates differed. We could grade a submitted program on correctness and objective value, and look back at other eligible commits to estimate how much score the selection left on the table.

The five-update pilot drew eight team episodes per training task. We kept a small fixed probe of four tasks, two episodes each. In a blind review of 24 training pairs, the later teams explored better-separated directions in 14, worse-separated directions in three, with seven ties. The direction result did not carry over to the sampled probe pairs. In another matched training slice, the later teams sent more messages and spent more tokens. The task-matched training reward change was +0.016 with an interval from −0.07 to +0.10. More visible teamwork gave us behavior to inspect; it was weak evidence for better outcomes.

The next phase tried giving more credit to useful participants. We used the selected commit’s lineage and verification as credit signals, then enabled a team penalty for leaving a better eligible candidate unused. A lineage signal can recognize the agent that produced the adopted change. It also makes provenance matter: re-committing somebody else’s work can look like authorship unless you track where the code came from.

We called the difference between the best eligible candidate’s score and the submitted score selection regret. It has an obvious shortcut: a team can show zero regret by exposing only its final choice. We tracked how many candidates reached grading alongside regret. The count held steady in the measured windows; the gaps between candidates widened.

A team that finds stronger alternatives can incur more regret without changing how it picks. Our comparison with random selection accounted for part of that: the regret ratio went from 0.78 to 0.85 of random-choice regret, with noisy estimates across updates. The penalty did not lower the observed regret. The comparison does not isolate its effect, but it points to a problem that a better candidate pool alone cannot solve.

What the selected high-regret episodes showed

We examined 12 deliberately selected training episodes with high regret. Every one had a better commit visible before submission, and 10 of the selected submissions scored zero. Eleven ended early, frequently with peers still testing. Nine submitters knew of the better commit, and only one episode had explicit agreement. Sample-only checks sometimes gave identical results for candidates whose full scores were very different. This is a failure audit. It describes how bad choices occurred and does not estimate their prevalence.

We used language-model readers to compare behaviors that a program score cannot label directly: independent directions, same-input tests, use of peer evidence, and the choice to submit. We also wrote a parser for concrete actions. The execution judge supplied verifiable scores. The readers supplied process annotations, with disagreements and missing context checked against traces. We did not treat a reader’s preference as the answer’s correctness or as evidence that a process reward had worked.

We next continued the policy with only outcome reward, leaving the credit and regret readouts as diagnostics. At the handoff checkpoint, 56% of episodes ran or compared a peer’s commit before submitting, 47% did a same-input comparison, and 18% used their own tests in a comparison. The last figure had already been declining before we removed the auxiliary rewards. It would be misleading to attribute that decline to the new objective. That outcome-trained branch became our RL continuation control for the next SFT experiment.

We had been counting a retired protocol

Some of our cooperation counters stayed at zero. They came from an earlier version of the harness that recorded structured proposals, acknowledgments, objections and check receipts. The commit-based harness did not expose those tools. Its agents could express the same decisions as ordinary messages and Git commits, outside the event stream those counters read.

We went back to the active protocol. It asks agents to post their approach, attach an objective only when measured, name a best commit near the merge, and agree to a specific commit. These markers give a more direct way to find the decision turns in traces. A checker still has to verify that the associated action happened.

The difference was largest around choosing a program. The 9B usually announced an approach. It rarely posted a best commit or attached a measured objective. Flash produced those markers frequently under the regenerated harness. That makes the comparison useful for finding training targets. Because the audit sampled different policies and episodes, it does not establish a causal effect on score.

We also revised the prompt to make seat identity explicit and ask for a number only when the agent had a measurement. The automated count of one kind of unsupported score claim dropped sharply. A small blind reread still found unverified claims in seven of 12 selected messages from the later version, down from 11 of 12 in the earlier one. The detector missed agents relaying peers’ numbers as their own evidence. The prompt and policy changed together, so this was a calibration check on the detector and a reason to keep auditing the messages.

Even the audit needed its own check. One reader received an empty prompt extract for the 9B episodes and inferred that the agents never saw the procedure. The underlying episodes carried the procedure. The chart uses the direct marker counts, while the walkthrough uses concrete, observable actions from the selected excerpts. This kind of provenance matters when a trace reader sees only a filtered view of an episode.

Follow the evidence to the decision

In the Flash excerpt, a peer extracted the exact candidate commit, rebuilt it in a clean directory and ran the checker before endorsing it. In the 9B excerpt, an agent asked for objections near the merge point and interpreted the absence of an explicit objection as permission to submit. Both episodes contain the vocabulary of teamwork. Their decisions are grounded in different evidence.

To evaluate a peer’s contribution, I want a chain I can inspect. Which commit did the peer provide? Which inputs were used? Did the submitter consider that result before choosing? The judge can tell us the program’s quality after the episode. The chain tells us which information the team used while there was still time to change the answer.

That distinction matters for reward design. Awarding points for an agree: message could teach an agent to agree with itself. Awarding points for a reported obj= could teach it to copy somebody else’s number. Both can look like better coordination in a log. We need to check that the objective came from running the stated program and that the final choice can be traced to evidence available to the submitter.

We taught the submit call before we taught when to use it

We kept complete, cleaned agent histories as context and applied supervised loss to selected turns. We marked approach posts, measured commit messages, endorsements and best-candidate posts. We also kept actions that support them, such as reading a peer commit or running a measurement. Tool results stayed in context so the model could see the evidence behind the action it was asked to imitate.

We piloted language-model annotation for finding those supporting turns. On nine complete pilot episodes it recovered 94 of 99 exact-pattern marker turns and proposed additional matches. The overlap was a cross-check, not a ground-truth accuracy measure. For the first full run we used text-pattern anchors and explicit rules for preceding measurements and peer-commit reads. That version gave us a reproducible training set without depending on a model label for every turn.

In an earlier inventory, 95% of episodes contained a marker, yet the marker turns accounted for only 1.7% of sampled tokens. Filtering whole episodes would have kept most of the original supervision. Adding the supporting actions and masking remaining wall-time turns gave the first build a 7.4% loss share. This is a change in what the student is asked to imitate. It does not make the long context free.

After filtering for genuine episodes and masking leaked portions, we trained on 525 episodes. The first checkpoint scored zero on a four-task panel with two episodes per task. Four episodes never submitted and the other four submitted code that failed to compile. One agent wrote a plausible plan, then spent 57K tokens thinking in a single turn. It exhausted its budget without compiling, testing or committing.

Most of the loss tokens in those selected turns were still Flash reasoning. We reran the same training recipe and kept loss only on messages and tool calls. In the eight-episode panel, the 90th percentile of thinking length fell from 8,675 to 62 tokens per call. The team submitted explicitly in seven of eight episodes and scored 0.188. The reference team scored 0.461 and submitted all eight. The small panel and residual trainer–serving discrepancy limit how much we can attribute to the mask, but we had enough evidence to hold the planned RL follow-up.

One trace posted the literal agreement template with a placeholder for the commit. Another asked a peer to rerun a candidate and proceeded without a reply. We also caught a counting bug that initially reported eight submissions. The harness had graded a fallback worktree in the remaining episode. We switched the count to explicit submit events so we could measure the behavior independently of the grade.

What can the 9B do with a Flash team’s work in progress?

We had seen agents fork, post an approach and submit. To find out which part needed help, we paused Flash teams at successive milestones and let the RL-trained 9B team finish their work. We restored each seat’s tool and message history, its inbox and worktree, and the shared commits. In the main comparison we stripped Flash’s private reasoning and preserved the fraction of each seat’s token budget already spent. The 9B chose its own next actions from there.

After inheriting the forks and initial approaches, the 9B scored near its start-from-scratch baseline. It still scored near that range after inheriting the first build. At that cut, 88% of episodes built at least two new candidates, yet scores stayed low. The continuation score rose from 0.212 to 0.508 when the handoff included numeric objectives from two Flash agents. That cut gave the student both more implementation work and new measurements. Their individual contributions remain open.

Choosing a candidate still mattered. In the later handoffs, the 9B sometimes submitted its own candidate even when a better inherited commit was still in Git. An early detector counted edits to an inherited worktree as destructive overwrites. A trace reread showed that those edits could leave the commit available. We changed the audit to ask which commit was submitted and whether a measured comparison justified the switch.

What the budget and environment checks changed

The earliest handoff had 43 wall-clock timeouts in 136 episodes, typically with token budget left. Excluding those episodes raised its mean from 0.180 to 0.214 and left the largest gap between the first build and the two measured candidates. The remaining early-cut estimate still depends on the wall cap.

We also checked a portable version of the workspace sandbox on eight selected cells. All 32 episodes ran, and the same commits got the same hidden scores. The paired score was lower under the new setup and more episodes timed out. We kept the main comparison within one environment.

Better feedback changed the edits more than the scores

First we asked whether the 9B could identify promising parts of its own build transcripts when it saw Flash’s progress. We scored the same sampled tokens with a frozen 9B policy, once with the extra context and once without it. The best of six score-ranking checks had an AUC of 0.573, and the highest-scoring spans pointed toward Flash’s approach about as often as random spans in a blinded read. We held the training pilot.

Then we gave the 9B direct, no-code feedback on one of its own candidates. Across 24 tasks, we compared a teacher critique with a generic request to improve and a sham critique. The teacher critique prompted more changes of approach. It scored 0.012 below the generic request and 0.001 below the sham, with both paired intervals spanning zero. All three arms finished below their starting candidates on average. Rewrites were especially prone to losing score, so the trace suggests keeping the incumbent program available while testing a proposed revision.

Starting from the base weights recovered some of the gap

We also tried full supervised fine-tuning from the pretrained 9B base weights. The first mix used 146 regenerated Flash episodes, split into 489 agent histories with 35.4 million supervised tokens per pass. The raw base averaged 0.030 on a ten-task smoke test with one attempt per task. The 26-task, five-attempt panel measures what more SFT epochs changed. Estimating uplift over raw base would require evaluating it on that same panel.

More passes improved score and nearly eliminated malformed tool calls. Explicit submissions barely moved, and more episodes ran out of tokens. At epoch eight, 29 final answers errored at grading and another 15 episodes produced no gradeable submission. The outcome-trained 9B team was still ahead on 21 of the 26 tasks. Even among scored answers, the SFT team averaged 0.103 against 0.159 for the outcome-trained reference. The team needed to get more working programs to grading and improve the programs that already reached it.

A separate comparison used 40 fixed FrontierCS tasks with five team attempts each. Flash scored 56.17 Avg@5 and 71.96 Best@5 on a 0–100 scale. The outcome-trained 9B team scored 11.52 and 17.41. It submitted in all 200 episodes, while Flash submitted in 94%. The 9B’s submission rate was already high on that panel, and Flash still produced far stronger programs.

We started a second round of base SFT from epoch eight. We added 228 episodes from one Flash regeneration batch and 436 from another, keeping graded, submitted episodes with positive scores. That brought the full mix to 810 episodes and 216.6 million supervised tokens per pass. The next held-out read was still pending on November 14. On the fixed panel we’ll check whether agents reach the merge with working candidates, compare those candidates on shared inputs, and preserve the best measured commit through submission.
