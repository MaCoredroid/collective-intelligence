Where a 9B Agent Swarm Gets Stuck

Teaching coding agents to build, test, and choose a solution

We tested four ways to train a 9B agent swarm on FrontierCS. The first supervised checkpoint faced different rules at evaluation and submitted nothing in 197 rollouts. The direct reinforcement learning checkpoint submitted reliably and gave us a reference. After regenerating Flash demonstrations in the 9B harness, we recovered most submissions in a small test by training on tools and messages. Full fine-tuning from the pretrained base improved across checkpoints and still trailed the swarm trained with reinforcement learning. Handoff experiments located the largest score gap around building and measuring competing programs.

Models used

  • Pretrained 9B base · Qwen3.5-9B-Base. Qwen’s dense, 9-billion-parameter language model before instruction and chat post-training. We used these weights, with the post-trained release’s tokenizer files, for our full supervised fine-tuning (SFT) run. Base model card
  • Qwen’s post-trained 9B · Qwen3.5-9B. The released chat and tool-use version of the same 9B architecture. It was the starting point for our direct reinforcement learning (RL) and for the initial and targeted SFT runs. Post-trained model card
  • Flash · Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-FP8. The teacher that generated the demonstrations. FP8 refers to the weight format. Flash model card
  • 27B critic · Qwen3.8-27B-FP8. Qwen’s dense, 27-billion-parameter model in FP8. It gave text feedback on the 9B’s candidate programs in the critique experiment. 27B model card

What we trained

Can an agent swarm learn to work together on FrontierCS? Here, every agent in the swarm runs the same 9B coding model. Each agent can fork a teammate into its own workspace, message peers with scores and commit references, and submit one Git commit for the whole team. The shared goal is to build working candidates, compare them on shared inputs, and choose one for grading.

  1. Supervised fine-tuning followed by reinforcement learning stalled when the first checkpoint submitted nothing in the evaluation harness.
  2. Direct reinforcement learning gave us a trained reference that submitted consistently while scoring well below Flash.
  3. Supervised fine-tuning on regenerated Flash traces recovered most submissions in a small test after we limited the loss to tools and messages. We held the planned RL follow-up.
  4. Full supervised fine-tuning from the pretrained 9B base improved scores across checkpoints and still trailed the outcome-trained team.

Part 8 · One Harness, Six Ways to Work

Initial training with SFT, then RL

We ran 53 SFT updates from Qwen’s post-trained 9B on complete Flash team demonstrations. The training set contained 631 episodes, including traces from our Flash RL run, split into 2,322 agent histories with 158 million assistant tokens and 245 million tokens including context. We held out the probe panel and the FrontierCS evaluation set. We used the step-53 SFT checkpoint to start the RL phase.

The training and evaluation harnesses differed

The original Flash traces used an older harness. Agents worked against a wall clock and followed a different submission routine. The 9B evaluation gave each agent a budget of 100,000 generated tokens, sent merge notices, and used a two-step submission. The first submit_final call shows the team’s state. The second selects the commit and ends the episode when called with confirm: true.

Evaluation and decision

The step-53 SFT checkpoint submitted nothing in 197 RL rollouts. In the sampled run, 27% of turns lacked a tool call, often because the model never closed its reasoning block. The model averaged about nine malformed calls per episode. Every attempt in the first RL update earned zero, leaving the group-relative objective with no gradient.

We regenerated the Flash traces in the 9B evaluation harness. That let Flash demonstrate how to allocate a token budget, respond to merge notices, and confirm the final commit. The failed rollout also gave us two things to check at the next SFT checkpoint. Could the student keep its reasoning within budget, and could it produce valid tool calls through submission?

Direct reinforcement learning on team outcomes

This experiment began before the first SFT run. We started from Qwen’s post-trained 9B in the team harness, where we could grade the submitted program for correctness and objective value. The first pilot used the verified team outcome and a term for which candidate the team selected. We also scored other eligible commits to see whether the team had found a better option.

Early checkpoint evaluation

The pilot generated eight team episodes per task from an 18-task training pool and ran for five RL updates. A blind review of 24 matched training pairs found more distinct approaches in 14, less distinct approaches in three, and seven ties. We saw no clear difference on the fixed probe. The later teams sent more messages and spent more tokens in a matched training slice. Its reward change was +0.016, with an interval from −0.07 to +0.10.

Reward changes for using a teammate’s work

We used the pilot’s step-5 RL checkpoint to start the continuation run. On its new, judge-scored team rollouts, we tried giving credit to agents who produced or verified the selected commit. We also penalized the gap between the best eligible score and the submitted score, which we called selection regret. A team can show zero regret by exposing only its final choice, so we tracked how many candidates reached grading as well. That count stayed flat in the measured windows while the scores of the best and submitted candidates moved apart.

Relative to random selection from the same candidate pools, regret rose from 0.78 to 0.85 of the random-choice value, with noisy estimates across updates. The penalty showed no clear improvement in selection. In twelve episodes selected for high regret, a better commit was visible before submission in every case, and eleven episodes ended early.

How we audited the high-regret episodes

Ten submitted programs scored zero. Nine submitters knew about the better commit and only one episode showed explicit agreement. Checks on the supplied examples sometimes gave the same result for candidates whose hidden scores were quite different. We used language-model readers to annotate decisions such as same-input testing and use of peer evidence, then checked their annotations against tool records. The execution judge supplied the scores.

Outcome-only checkpoint

After step 20 of the continuation run, we trained on fresh team rollouts using only the submitted program’s score as reward. We kept the other signals as diagnostics. At step 66 of this run, 56% of episodes ran or compared a peer’s commit, 47% compared programs on the same inputs, and 18% used their own tests in a comparison. Use of those tests was already declining before the reward change.

On a separate panel of 40 FrontierCS tasks with five attempts per task, the trained 9B submitted in all 200 episodes. Flash submitted in 94%. The 9B averaged 11.52 across attempts and 17.41 across the best of five. Flash reached 56.17 and 71.96 on the same 0–100 scale. This checkpoint gave us a reference for further training. Submission was already reliable, and program quality was the larger gap to Flash.

Targeted SFT on regenerated Flash traces

With Flash now running in the 9B harness, we could look for the behavior we wanted the student to imitate. The main question was which parts of the long demonstrations should carry training loss. We wanted the student to learn the actions that lead to a tested commit while keeping the tool results that explain those actions in context.

What we found while collecting and cleaning the data

Early counters looked for retired proposal and acknowledgment tools. In the current harness, agents communicate through ordinary messages and commit references. We looked for approach posts, measured objectives, endorsements and best-candidate messages, then checked the tool records for the runs behind them. The trained 9B usually posted an approach. Flash more often posted a measured score or named a best commit.

In an early trace inventory, 95% of episodes contained a protocol marker, and marker turns accounted for only 1.7% of sampled tokens. Filtering whole episodes would have left most of the same training targets. We selected the marker turns and the actions leading up to them, such as rebuilding a peer’s commit or running the test that produced a score.

Of 608 regenerated episodes, 564 survived initial cleaning and 539 contained a marker. We used pattern-based rules to select turns for loss and language-model annotations to filter out agent histories that skipped the procedure or posted its markers without the underlying work. Dropping every history flagged for leakage would have removed 35% of the remaining loss tokens. We kept those histories and masked the flagged turns out of the loss. The final set covered 525 episodes.

How we checked the trace labels

A Flash peer rebuilt the exact commit in a clean directory and ran the checker before endorsing it. In a 9B trace, a submitter asked for objections and treated silence as agreement. Those examples explain why we checked the test record and the commit behind each message. In a pilot of nine episodes, the annotator recovered 94 of 99 pattern-matched turns and identified additional candidates.

Checks on the prompt and annotation

Making seat identity and measurement requirements explicit in the prompt reduced the automated count of one type of unsupported score claim. A small blind reread found unverified claims in seven of 12 later messages, down from 11 of 12 earlier messages. The detector missed some agents repeating a peer’s score as their own. A separate audit error came from an empty prompt extract. The underlying episodes did carry the procedure.

What changed between the two checkpoints

Both SFT runs started from Qwen’s post-trained 9B and trained for 40 updates on the same 525 regenerated Flash episodes. The first applied loss to reasoning, messages, and tool calls on selected turns. About 80% of its 9.38 million loss tokens were Flash reasoning. One agent spent 57,000 tokens thinking in a single call and exhausted its budget before compiling, testing, or committing. The second run applied loss only to messages and tool calls.

All four submissions from the first checkpoint failed to compile. The second checkpoint produced valid calls and restored most submissions. Teams still submitted early, around 37% of the way through their token budget, with the merge cue scheduled at 80%. One trace posted an agreement with a placeholder for the commit. Another asked for a peer rerun and proceeded without the result. We also found that the harness could grade a fallback worktree without an explicit submit call, so we counted the calls separately from the grades.

Decision. We held the planned RL follow-up. The step-40 tool-and-message checkpoint scored below the step-5 RL pilot, and the panel was small with a residual trainer–serving discrepancy. Recovering the call itself gave us a clearer target for the next run. The team still needed to produce and choose stronger programs.

Full SFT from the pretrained 9B base

We ran a separate full SFT from Qwen3.5-9B-Base on 146 regenerated Flash episodes, split into 489 agent histories, with 35.4 million supervised tokens per pass. The raw base averaged 0.030 on a ten-task smoke test with one attempt per task. For the main evaluation, we compared the checkpoint after 78 updates (epoch 3) with the one after 130 more updates on the same data (epoch 8). Both ran on the same 26 tasks with five attempts each.

What improved across checkpoints

From epoch three to epoch eight, the average score rose from 0.029 to 0.067 and malformed tool calls nearly disappeared. Explicit submissions barely moved and more episodes ran out of tokens. At epoch eight, 29 final answers errored at grading and another 15 episodes had no gradeable submission. Even among scored answers, the SFT team averaged 0.103 against 0.159 for the outcome-trained reference. That reference led on 21 of the 26 tasks. The raw-base smoke test used a different sample, so the epoch-to-epoch result is the matched comparison.

What we changed for the next round

We resumed from epoch eight with two more batches of Flash demonstrations collected in the same harness. We added 228 episodes from one batch and 436 from the other, keeping submitted episodes with positive grades. The new mix had 810 episodes and 216.6 million supervised tokens per pass. A held-out evaluation of that round was still pending at the November 14 evidence cutoff.

Where the 9B team lost score

The checkpoint evaluations showed that a team could submit reliably and still score poorly. To locate the gap, we paused Flash teams at successive milestones and let the step-66 team RL checkpoint finish. We restored the worktrees, commits, tool history and messages, and preserved the fraction of each agent’s budget that had already been spent. The main comparison stripped Flash’s private reasoning from the inherited state.

Inheriting forks, plans or a first build left the 9B near its start-from-scratch score. From the first-build handoff, 88% of episodes built at least two new candidates and still scored low. The mean rose from 0.212 to 0.508 when the handoff included numeric objectives from two Flash agents. That cut supplied both implementation work and measurements, so their individual contributions remain open.

Later in the episode, the 9B sometimes picked its own commit while a better inherited one was still in Git. An early detector interpreted edits to an inherited worktree as overwrites. We switched the audit to the commit that actually went to grading and checked whether a comparison justified the choice.

Budget and environment checks

The earliest handoff had 43 wall-clock timeouts in 136 episodes, typically with token budget left. Excluding those episodes raised its mean from 0.180 to 0.214 and left the largest gap between the first build and the two measured candidates. The remaining early-cut estimate still depends on the wall cap.

A portable workspace sandbox ran all 32 episodes across eight selected cells. The same commits received the same hidden scores. The paired team score was lower and more episodes timed out, so we kept the main comparison within one environment.

Could teacher feedback close the gap?

We gave the frozen step-66 team RL checkpoint Flash’s progress as extra context and asked whether its token scores could identify promising parts of its own build traces. The best of six ranking checks gave an AUC of 0.573. The top-ranked spans pointed toward Flash’s approach about as often as randomly selected spans in a blind read. We held the training pilot.

On 24 tasks, we used Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 to critique a candidate from the same step-66 checkpoint, with no code in the critique. The checkpoint then revised its candidate. Critique prompted more changes of approach but showed no clear score advantage over a generic request to improve or a sham critique. All three arms scored below their starting candidates on average. Rewrites were especially prone to losing score.

What we learned

Collect demonstrations in the harness where the student will run. The first SFT run exposed how budget cues and submission rules can diverge between training and evaluation. Matching the harness made it possible to teach the right actions, and limiting the loss to those actions recovered most submissions on the small panel.

Grade submission and program quality separately. The direct RL checkpoint submitted consistently, and more supervised training improved tool use. The gap to Flash remained large. The handoffs narrowed the next question to what happens before submission. On the fixed evaluation panel we’ll measure whether agents arrive at the merge with working candidates, compare them on shared inputs, and submit the best commit they actually measured.
