What Did the Teacher Actually Transfer?

On-policy distillation from Qwen Flash into a 9B coding model

I asked a larger teacher to score the 9B’s own reasoning and code on FrontierCS. Average score rose, while the share of tasks with any positive score stayed at half. The gains were concentrated where the student already worked with shorter reasoning, and many more attempts ran to the thinking cap. That split changed what I wanted the teacher to teach next.

Five attempts tell three different stories

In Part 7, we tried to teach a 9B coding model advanced search, including simulated annealing. The student could imitate a strong teacher’s long derivation and still produce a program that failed to compile. This time, I wanted the teacher to see the states the student would actually encounter. We let the 9B write its own reasoning, asked a larger Qwen Flash teacher to score it token by token, and updated the student with that signal.

The strongest measured OPD run improved the average of five attempts by 1.98 points over the Flash-SFT starting checkpoint. It also improved compilation by about six percentage points. The best attempt out of five moved only 0.62 points, with substantial uncertainty. Half the tasks earned some credit before training and half did afterward. These are three different questions about a model that will be sampled repeatedly.

Avg@5 tells me what a randomly selected attempt is worth on average. Best@5 asks how much a scorer can recover if I have five attempts to choose from. Coverage counts tasks with at least one positive score. Here a positive score means some benchmark credit. A task can earn credit with a simple construction, and better optimization can raise the score without introducing a new algorithm.

The evaluation uses our frozen 172-task FrontierCS packet and one-shot code generation. Each checkpoint generates five programs per task with a 98,304-token thinking cap. One unavailable grade in the OPD packet was counted as zero. The benchmark provides executable scores for open-ended programs, which lets us track quality across attempts without treating every positive score as a full solution.

There are two baselines. The immediate starting policy is the third epoch of Flash SFT: 1,110 demonstrations from 268 tasks, with thinking and code given equal loss weight. A separate 9B policy trained with direct RL from the base reached 13.91 Avg@5 and 21.65 Best@5, with 60.5% coverage. I use the SFT baseline to ask what the teacher signal changed and the direct RL baseline to ask what another training path bought.

Part 7 · The Distance to a Teacher · FrontierCS benchmark

The teacher reads the student’s prefix

Supervised fine-tuning gives the student a complete teacher demonstration. It learns to predict the next token with the teacher’s preceding text already in context. On-policy distillation changes who writes that context. The student samples a continuation, and the frozen teacher scores the tokens the student actually chose, conditioned on that same history.

For a sampled token, the local signal is log pteacher − log pstudent. Averaged over the student’s token choices at a given prefix, this is the negative reverse KL at that prefix. In the experiment, we score both thinking and code, mask special tokens, and keep a small reference penalty to the starting student. The teacher signal is available throughout the rollout, including on attempts that would never earn useful benchmark reward.

The difficult part is deciding how much credit to assign to a choice when its consequences come thousands of tokens later. In the strongest arm we add a weighted, discounted return of subsequent teacher signals. Think of that as a way to give a choice some credit for what follows it. When subsequent tokens are mostly penalized, a long continuation can carry a cumulative cost even if each token by itself carries a small one.

Recipe and measurement details

The starting policy was the third epoch of the earlier Flash SFT run. The larger teacher was Flash in FP8. We used a sampled-token reverse-KL signal with the teacher re-tempered to 0.8 using a top-logprob reconstruction with a tail estimate. The stronger-return arm used a coefficient of 0.01 and discount 0.9999 for the teacher return, learning rate 10−6, and a reference KL coefficient of 0.001. It used eight training tasks with eight student samples each per update and ran for five updates. Its objective did not include outcome reward.

A plain teacher-only arm at the same teacher temperature was near the SFT baseline on Avg@5 and fell 4.72 points on Best@5. The stronger-return arm improved the mean and recovered the best-of-five loss. Both arms encountered long rollouts. The run comparisons describe these specific recipes and checkpoints.

The first teacher signal made the student worse

Our first try used the teacher’s original temperature. On the one-sample screen, mean score fell from 7.58 to 3.64. Compile rate fell from 73.8% to 55.8%, and median thinking grew from 57.5K to 94.3K tokens. Adding verifiable outcome reward brought the score back to 4.43, with even more attempts reaching the thinking cap. Both paired score intervals were below zero. Outcome feedback could still distinguish finished programs, but it did little to rescue this recipe’s ability to reach one.

We sharpened the teacher to temperature 0.8. Early in training, one batch had student entropy close to the starting policy and still thought about 23K tokens longer. A model can be just as decisive at each token and keep making decisions for much longer. On five samples, that arm came back to the SFT mean, but the best attempt fell by 4.72 points and roughly 72% of attempts hit the cap.

Adding the stronger discounted return changed the curve. Compared with SFT, it improved the expected first draw by 1.98 points, then gave up most of that margin as more samples were available. Compared with plain 0.8 OPD, it recovered much of the upper tail. This was the recipe we kept. It was also a reason to look inside the rollout: we had a teacher signal that improved answers on average while sending many more attempts to the cap.

Once the student starts repeating, the next token is easy to predict

Imagine a student that has settled on a candidate solution. It repeats its complexity analysis, then repeats the same plan in slightly different words. The teacher is now judging a familiar continuation to a repetitive prefix. It can assign high probability to another repeat even if its own independent solution would have moved on.

We saw this in the rollout audit. Repeat lines often carried a much smaller teacher–student log-probability gap than first-occurrence reasoning. In the audited batches, retaining repeated lines reduced the mean per-token penalty relative to computing the mean without those lines. The effect is a property of the tokens and the reduction rule. It does not identify why any particular sample entered a loop.

That split helps explain why the improvement was hard to use. When the distilled model closed its reasoning on its own, its programs scored nearly twice as well on average as the starting student’s naturally closed programs. At the same time, forced closes rose from about 24% to 57% of attempts. In a group of five we went from about 3.8 natural closes to 2.1. Some forced answers still scored, but we had fewer of the kind that tended to score well.

We also looked for the point where more thinking stopped helping. In 16 selected traces, we used the final answer to find an earlier code block already close to the emitted program. On review, 15 looked ready to finish and 14 spent further tokens without a useful change. The median extra reasoning was about 15K tokens. At those points the teacher gave the closing token a median probability near one in a million. The first code block was usually much less like the final program, so closing at the first sight of code would be a bad rule. This audit uses hindsight to find a decision point; it gives us a test case, not a detector we can use at inference.

We tried cutting the loop in two places. Cutting its tail out of the training return also removed negative return that had reached the decisions just before loop onset. On one audited batch the pre-loop return moved from −7.94 to −0.37 under the cut. That made the lead-in cheaper under this objective, and the training variant continued to loop. The result is specific to how we formed and masked the return.

We also changed decoding on the same checkpoint. Tightening the sampling distribution to the teacher’s card settings increased repetition and forced closes in a one-sample sweep. Cutting repetition at inference cut thinking length, but the run still scored about seven points on average against 10.33 with the original sampler. Ending a loop early saved tokens without restoring the original score in this screen.

Perhaps the policy simply needed to train under the sampler it would use at test time. We continued for three updates using the coding sampler with a thinking-only presence penalty, then evaluated both checkpoints with that sampler. The score moved from 7.33 to 6.27 and forced closes from 65.1% to 79.1%. The score interval still crossed zero. The original sampler also gave a lower point estimate after retraining. Matching train and test sampling was a reasonable fix to try; it did not produce a recovery here.

Two more changes separated exposure from skill. Continuing with a 64K training cap gave a 5.48 one-sample score under the usual 98K evaluation cap, versus 10.33 before that continuation. The cap controlled what the policy saw during training. It did not provide a label for when to finish. A windowed experiment generated rollouts but stopped before a completed update, so it has no held-out result.

The gain came mostly from tasks the student already handled quickly

I grouped tasks by how long the starting 9B thought, using the median of its five attempts. This fixes the grouping before looking at the distilled model’s score. The largest improvement came from tasks where the starting student took fewer than 16,000 thinking tokens.

The teacher can improve decisions within a path the student already visits. On tasks where the student often fills its reasoning window, the local signal can see many tokens inside a failing path. A useful correction might live at an early choice of algorithm, far away from the point where the judge reports a zero. The score-by-length plot is consistent with this explanation. A paired program audit would be needed to identify which algorithm families actually changed.

There is another clue in the audit. Within a group of attempts on the same task, the teacher’s answer-token signal correlated modestly with the judge score, around +0.20 in the stronger-return arm. The aggregate thinking-token signal was about +0.07, with uncertainty including zero. That average can wash out an important early token or reflect how long the model stays in a familiar loop. I would use the answer-side signal as a diagnostic and inspect decision points directly before treating either aggregate as a process score.

What did the extra training buy?

We carried the improved checkpoint into longer experiments. The hybrid kept teacher feedback and added verifiable outcomes. It reached 9.21 Avg@5 and later fell to 8.83. The gain at the earlier checkpoint was small relative to the uncertainty. It looked more encouraging on selected training tasks than on the held-out packet.

The alternating run separated the signals into blocks. After 20 outcome-RL updates, it scored 8.34 Avg@5 and brought forced closes down to 32.3%. Five more OPD updates moved the score to 8.69, with longer thinking and more forced closes again. The paired change was +0.35 with a 95% interval of −0.78 to +1.51. A later RL leg reached 9.18. Alternating let us see the stopping behavior move as we changed objectives, but the best mean remained close to the original five-update checkpoint.

A separate supervised anchor added likelihood training on teacher answers. Early rollouts stopped more often, which was the immediate goal. On held-out one-sample screens, mean score fell from 8.16 at the first readout to 5.78 and 5.24 at later ones. These are separate from the five-sample hybrid results. Teaching from a good answer made some trajectories easier to close; we still needed the policy’s own continuation to produce a good program.

The direct-RL line starts from the same 9B base family and uses a different training path. At a nearby point in the ledger, the alternating run’s 436 million tokens were compared with a direct-RL checkpoint at about 480 million. The alternating run was 4.31 Avg@5 points lower, with a task-paired 95% interval from −6.54 to −2.29. The later direct-RL checkpoint scored 13.91. These totals are useful for deciding what to try next. Different rollout lengths, model serving costs and data curricula make them an imperfect comparison of wall time or accelerator cost.

I would separate the comparison with the immediate starting checkpoint from the comparison with an alternative use of the budget. The first tells us that the teacher signal improved this student’s average sample. The second asks whether this path was the best way to spend the training effort from the base. In these runs, direct RL was ahead on the latter comparison. We started setting up two follow-ups: skip SFT and distill from a stronger RL checkpoint into the base 9B before outcome RL, and use Flash feedback on a student already strengthened by RL. The first was still at a short mechanics check; neither had an efficacy result in this snapshot.

Measure the algorithm before measuring the climb

The capability I wanted was more specific than a higher score. Can the 9B write a correct implementation of simulated annealing or another advanced search procedure on a task where the starting policy seldom does? And once it has a working implementation, can it use feedback to improve the objective while preserving correctness?

I would split the next curriculum into those two skills. First ask for a single correct program, and inspect the actual search loop, objective delta and acceptance rule. Count correct implementations across several samples per held-out task and record which approaches the starting model already produces. Then give both policies the same verified starting program, submission feedback and search budget. Measure the score improvement and correctness of each accepted edit.

Our earlier one-shot audit shows why those checks need to be explicit. A clock read that looked like time-budgeted search was being used to seed the random number generator. There was real fixed-iteration local search elsewhere. A useful audit distinguishes fixed iterations, a clock that actually controls search, and a working annealing schedule that sometimes accepts a worse move. They are different capabilities and can earn similar program scores on a single input.

That design makes success visible at the point where it matters. Better one-shot construction would show up in the programs themselves. Better hill climbing would show up in a sequence of valid, measured improvements from the same starting point. The November 8 snapshot contains a stronger average OPD sample and a persistent stopping problem. The algorithm-level read is the next test.
