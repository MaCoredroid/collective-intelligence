The Distance to a Teacher

Distilling advanced search into a 9B coding model

A strong teacher is not necessarily an easy teacher to learn from. We tried off-policy SFT, OPD and RL to teach a 9B model to construct and improve advanced search algorithms on FrontierCS. The matched evaluations separate copied style, reliable programs and occasional strong answers. New controls show why length drift alone cannot decide whether distillation helped.

First ask what actually transferred

The missing matched evaluation has now landed. Flash-SFT e3 was evaluated with the same original prompt and 172-problem ×5 packet as the strongest earlier RL checkpoint. It is much less reliable per sample, but its best-of-five score comes close.

Here, Avg@5 means the task-averaged mean of five scores; Best@5 means the task-averaged maximum. They measure different things. A sparse tail of good answers can coexist with poor average reliability. Nonzero credit is also a much weaker condition than a fully solved task.

The earlier small-seed SFT → RL branch showed the same need for separate readouts: checkpoint 114 compiled more often than 84 and produced some timed loops, but lost 2.96 average-score points on the matched packet. None of these aggregate metrics alone certifies correct simulated annealing.

A demonstration can change what the student sometimes produces without making that capability reliable.

What we train on changes what the teacher can teach

The desired skill is concrete: write a correct solver that can use simulated annealing, local search or another advanced algorithm, then improve that solver from execution feedback. In our earlier samples, the 9B rarely produced the target time-budgeted search behavior. Fixed-iteration local search did exist. The gap is reliable implementation of stronger search, rather than knowledge of the words.

We used three different learning signals. Off-policy SFT copies a saved teacher response. On-policy distillation (OPD) scores a response sampled by the current student. Verifiable-reward RL scores what the resulting program actually does. They expose different mistakes.

This round deliberately narrowed the episode to one complete program per response. It does not yet teach the whole long-horizon agent loop of editing, submitting, reading feedback and surviving compactions. It tries to establish a construction skill that the longer loop can improve.

That gives the curriculum two separate tests: can the model generate a sound advanced solver in one shot, and can it hill-climb that solver without breaking correctness? Neither a good imitation loss nor a higher aggregate RL reward answers both.

The student found the teacher’s voice first

I then tried a larger set of Kimi K3 demonstrations. The 9B adopted terse planning and short thinking. Its final programs became much smaller than the teacher’s, often with undeclared names, type errors or unfinished sketches.

On FrontierCS, that SFT checkpoint scored 0.33 Avg@5 and compiled only 21.9% of its answers. Passing teacher demonstrations were not enough to make the student generate passing programs.

Look at where the loss goes

The original K3 run gave each trace equal overall weight, then averaged its supervised tokens. Thinking occupied most of those tokens and was much harder for the student to predict. It accounted for nearly all the measured loss.

That is a loss-accounting fact, not a measurement of gradient importance. It still changes the practical question: how much of the objective is directly teaching the final program?

The next recipe averaged thinking and answer losses separately:

L = ½ mean(NLLthinking) + ½ mean(NLLanswer)

Equal coefficients remove the token-count imbalance. They do not make the two numerical losses—or their gradients—equal. More importantly, the balanced K3 retry still compiled only 7 of 84 dev outputs after one epoch. Weighting was a necessary design choice to inspect, not a demonstrated cure.

Choose demonstrations the student can reach

The next useful measurement came before training: score each teacher’s demonstrations under the starting student. The Qwen Flash set was much closer to the 9B than the K3 set, for both thinking and code.

This is more informative than choosing a teacher only by its own benchmark score. But it is not a clean test of model family: prompting, selected problems, code length and which traces survived the filters also differed.

We kept distinct passing programs, filtered answer-code placeholders, checked tokenizer round trips and full lengths, and inspected the actual first-batch loss. Those checks made the training target reviewable. They did not establish a winning recipe.

The teacher’s skill and the student’s ability to absorb its demonstrations are different quantities.

What the Flash data and loss checks actually establish

The kept set covered 275 of 291 generation problems. The training filter left 1,110 traces on 268 problems. The balanced first-step loss matched its expected value, 0.3684. The source later reported thinking still contributed 88% of numerical loss under balanced coefficients; equal weighting is not equal realized loss. The cancelled code-only experiment produced no checkpoint, so it supplies no positive evidence for answer-only SFT.

The SFT attempts, including the ones that did not help

The mitigation sequence was not “more of the same data.” We changed which traces survived, which tokens carried loss, how far the teacher was from the student, and how long we trained. The table keeps those changes and their outcomes together.

Selection made the data cleaner, not automatically teachable

For K3, we kept up to eight distinct passing programs per problem. A trace had to finish naturally, pass all checked cases, score above zero and fit the sequence limits. Duplicate programs within a problem were collapsed. The training build also checked tokenizer round trips and prompt + thinking + answer length.

The K3 limits were 65,536 thinking tokens, 32,768 answer tokens and 139,264 total tokens. The longest retained full sequence was about 106K tokens. All 1,704 examples were trained; the later collapse was not explained by silently dropping long traces.

For the balanced retries, we removed final answers containing literal ... placeholders and the tenth of answers with highest starting NLL. Sketches inside the thinking were retained. This reduced one obvious source of broken code, but also changed the difficulty distribution: the removed traces were not a random holdout.

The dataset count hides two different kinds of coverage

Eight solutions to one problem can provide algorithmic variety. They are still eight examples of the same problem. The K3 set’s 1,704 traces covered 278 problems; the final Flash training set’s 1,110 traces covered 268. Trace count and independent task coverage should be reported separately.

Long traces add many prediction targets, including planning prose and intermediate code sketches. More tokens do not guarantee more examples of the capability we want. Here, the student sometimes moved sketches from the thinking into the final program. That is a concrete form of target ambiguity to inspect, rather than evidence that every long trace is noisy.

Clipping was a diagnosis, not a verdict

The first K3 run clipped 103 of 104 steps. We retried with a lower learning rate, but clipping tests the gradient norm before that learning rate scales the update. In a later window, norms were mostly near the 1.0 threshold; a norm of 1.21 meant a roughly 17% reduction. The clipping rate alone did not establish instability, and lowering LR did not rescue the measured dev compilation result.

The useful practice is to verify the loss and inspect free generation. Filtering, balanced coefficients and a smaller learning rate are interventions to test, not a checklist that guarantees transfer.

RL can repair code while removing the target behavior

After Flash SFT, early RL shortened repeated drafts and improved compilation. On recurring training problems, compile rate rose from 54% to 65%, while scores among compiled programs stayed almost flat: 26.2 → 26.4. The apparent lead over earlier pure RL was mostly easier compilation.

Another continuation added search bonuses and near-miss credit. Later, reward rose while timed search vanished. A bonus that pays only qualifying search cannot help once the policy stops sampling any.

The curriculum still needs two gates: first generate a correct advanced algorithm often enough to learn from it; then reward improvements that preserve that construction. The current runs do not show that those gates have both been cleared.

The fixes we tried were aimed at different failure modes

A compile-check sentence in the prompt barely moved search-attempt compile failures in the first 192-sample batch: 69% → 66% on the trained rows, within the uncertainty of that small comparison. It was not a demonstrated repair for names, types or inconsistent program state.

Near-miss credit tried to recognize repairable failed search. Static guards tried to prevent paying a timer wrapped around a non-improving construction. The later increase from a 0.05 to a 0.15 search bonus still paid nothing once the policy stopped sampling qualifying programs. Those mechanisms changed incentives, but did not establish preservation of the target behavior.

We also considered removing negative advantage from failed search attempts. A plain floor was rejected: adding a superficial timer to broken code could earn protection from a penalty. An offline replay can expose that incentive, but it cannot predict what a newly trained policy will sample.

A rollback with a much stronger reference anchor was built later. That is a proposed preservation intervention, not a positive result in this article. The central bottleneck remains whether correct advanced-search programs occur often enough to learn from.

OPD had already taught us to watch the stopping boundary

OPD was not a fresh switch we assumed would fix SFT. Earlier attempts had already produced a recurring failure: the student kept thinking, then ran out of room to write a usable answer. One lower-learning-rate variant kept length stable, so we reused its general recipe for the Flash-teacher attempt.

The distinction matters for early stopping. A clean intermediate checkpoint can be more useful than the last one: in the stable variant, step 4 beat step 6. We therefore saved every update and treated rising thinking length together with forced closure as a reason to stop, even if the teacher-divergence metric improved.

Stopping is part of the behavior being distilled

On-policy distillation lets the teacher score the student’s own sampled tokens. That addresses one conditioning gap, but introduces another control problem: learning when to end thinking.

Our Flash teacher generated shorter reasoning in a probe. Even so, the student’s OPD outputs grew rapidly. Lower teacher–student divergence did not guarantee a usable response length.

SimpleOPD motivates a student reference anchor and termination-token masking. When EOS Tokens Disagree shows why declared stopping sets can still hide probability mismatches. These are relevant mechanisms; neither paper validates our coding recipe.

What was already in the Flash OPD recipe

We started from Flash-SFT e3, used the original one-shot prompt, sampled 16 tasks ×4 completions per batch and took one update per batch at LR 3×10−7. The planned ten updates used 96K thinking and 32K answer caps. Teacher credit covered only the first 42,778 thinking tokens of each response, while answer tokens stayed included.

The teacher signal on the thinking-close and end-of-message targets was clamped to be nonnegative. This is weaker than removing the teacher term at those targets, and it is different from anchoring the student to its own starting termination probabilities. Despite the clamp and prefix cap, the first three batches drifted longer.

We checked shared token IDs, prompt rendering and next-token log-probability alignment on a 96K sequence. We also confirmed the actual LR: a readout had rounded 3×10−7 to 0.0. The optimizer step, nonzero gradient norm and changed weights ruled out that display error as a no-update explanation.

Teacher reverse-KL fell from 0.135 to 0.119 while forced closure rose from 6.3% to 34.4%. The desired teacher agreement and the undesired stopping drift happened together. A shorter-generating teacher, verified alignment, low LR and a bounded thinking prefix were therefore insufficient safeguards in this run.

What the corrected retries change

The new recipe zeros the teacher term on all special-token targets. Separately, a reference-KL penalty with coefficient 0.5 pulls toward frozen e3, including at those positions. The coefficient is a configuration choice; the actual KL contribution has to be measured as training proceeds.

Those retries retained the same low LR, batch shape, prefix cap and original stepwise stop rule. We ran them with anchor coefficients 0.5 and 2.0. Both initially triggered the old length rule; the updated section below reports the matched-task audit and first held-out quality check. Answer-only OPD was discussed but not run. It must not be confused with the cancelled answer-only SFT attempt.

The stop rule needed a control, too

The two mask-and-anchor retries subsequently ran. Both initially got longer. It was tempting to call that another OPD failure. A retrospective audit changed the interpretation.

All three OPD runs drew the same tasks at each step, but consecutive steps shared only one or two tasks. Even with unchanged e3 weights on the same sixteen tasks, a 64-sample draw’s thinking median ranged from 31.9K to 45.9K in the saved 95% noise band. The first rise in the KL-0.5 retry started from an unusually short baseline draw.

Matched-task reads still found real length growth by step 3, so the entire effect was not noise. But the KL-2.0 run reverted at step 4, and those matched comparisons covered only four to six tasks. Length was a useful alarm; it was not a sufficient quality verdict.

The revised rule compares each checkpoint with e3 on the same tasks and looks for harm: excess forced closure or reduced compilation across two successive steps, with extreme-failure backstops. We still log length and entropy. We no longer let an unmatched length median stand in for “this method works.”

The teacher’s sampling distribution became part of the recipe

We then tested a combined teacher-and-outcome objective. It still grew longer. An audit of code, token alignment, loss sign, masks and aggregation did not reveal a simple implementation error that accounted for the pattern. A proposed “fidelity cliff” was not supported either: token-level divergence did not predict compilation or score within a task.

The next hypothesis concerned the target distribution. The teacher’s raw probabilities and the distribution it actually samples after truncation are not the same target. A student can be pushed toward a more diffuse distribution even when that teacher’s own sampled answers are shorter.

The continuation’s third-batch comparison looked encouraging on eight matched tasks: compilation and reward exceeded e3. On the next eight tasks, both differences were within noise again. Entropy also rose sharply. That is an unresolved experiment, not a success to extrapolate.

The working practice is to specify the teacher distribution, keep a self-teacher control, and judge matched-task validity and reward alongside length. The top-20 change is a hypothesis under test.

The practices we would carry into the next attempt

Screen the transfer, not just the teacher.

Measure starting NLL by thinking and code, then test free generation. Easy teacher-conditioned code prediction can coexist with broken student-written programs.

Audit the objective that actually runs.

Check masks, normalization and first-batch loss. Inspect clipping magnitude rather than treating every clipped step as instability. Balanced coefficients are a control, not a success result.

Keep skill, correctness and reward on separate axes.

Show compilation, advanced-algorithm execution and held-out score. Read the programs behind any search proxy; a timer can bound a greedy construction.

Preserve a matched evaluation packet.

Prompt, sampler, thinking and answer caps, seeds and grader matter. The original-prompt Flash-SFT result now separates average reliability from Best@5. Changed-prompt runs remain separate comparisons.

Give drift an explicit stop rule.

Track thinking length and forced closure from the first OPD batch, alongside code quality. A self-teacher control and matched-task quality checks changed the interpretation of the early rises. The top-20-target continuation remains unresolved.

For the curriculum, a construction gate should check that generated programs execute the intended improvement loop, maintain their objective and best-so-far state, and write back a valid result. Only then does a feedback gate ask whether subsequent hill-climbing improves a matched result without discarding that structure. These are proposed acceptance criteria, not gates the current runs have already passed.

These runs produced better diagnostic habits. They have not yet produced a validated recipe for transferring reliable advanced search into the 9B.
